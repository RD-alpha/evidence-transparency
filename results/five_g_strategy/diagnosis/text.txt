S0680 Standards for 5G A technical standard is fundamentally an agreement between parties developing a technology to do things in a particular way.
S0681 Standards development is a business-led activity, drawing on ideas from academic research and commercial research and development.
S0682 In Information and Communication Technology (ICT), standards are essential commercial tools, without which there would be a proliferation of incompatible technologies.
S0683 For 5G a lack of widely agreed standardisation could mean phones that do not work on all networks or in all countries - as was the case 25 years ago - or base stations that do not meet regulators’ criteria.
S0684 Government and regulators are often involved in standards development, partly to contribute relevant knowledge but mainly to ensure that wider societal needs are addressed by standards.
S0685 These may, for example, include: security, spectrum protection, safety, interoperability, accessibility, environmental performance, and provision for government needs in products.
S0686 Establishing 5G standards will mainly follow the process that set standards for 2G, 3G, and 4G. They are being developed by a group of pan-regional standards bodies through the 3G Partnership Project (3GPP).
S0687 The European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI), the regional standards body for Europe, hosts the staff who work for 3GPP.
S0688 Once developed, the standards will be issued by ETSI and its regional partners across the world.
S0689 3GPP will follow two parallel strands of work on 5G: one to allow the use of existing 4G equipment (an evolutionary path), the other (longer-term) building on 4G standards initially but working towards an almost total replacement of them by new 5G standards.
S0690 Mobile phone standards sit within a global regulatory regime administered by the International Telecommunications Union (ITU – a United Nations specialised agency).
S0691 These are supplemented by national and regional requirements as appropriate.
S0692 The ITU’s International Mobile Telecommunications 2020 work (IMT 2020) draws together discussion and study of the developing 5G standards.
S0693 This will provide increasing clarity around the likely shape of 5G standards until it sets out the final definition for IMT - the formal 5G standard.
S0694 3GPP plans for two releases of 5G standards in 2018 (Release 15, largely an evolution of 4G) which will allow early adopters of 5G to have an agreed baseline and in 2020 (Release 16) a more comprehensive set of standards.
S0695 Standards for 5G will include a concept called New Radio (NR) that will describe a completely different radio system from 4G. NR will be better able to support massive data and low speed data, and be more spectrum efficient.
S0696 The initial standards release in mid-2018 will cover an evolution of the 4G core network, which provides interconnection between all the base stations and control of the overall network and users.
S0697 Further developments for the core network are expected to be based heavily on Software Defined Networks (SDN), where software controls elements of network function that were previously ‘baked into’ the hardware and could not be changed.
S0698 SDN will allow core networks to be readily built on general purpose computing systems and use cloud computing.
S0699 The recently developed Network Function Virtualisation (NFV) standards will be the implementation of SDN used as the starting point for new 5G core network standards.
S0700 Greater use of SDN may lead to new combinations of manufacturers and providers in the market.
S0701 Such standards work also has security implications.
S0703 Key issues to consider The ongoing 3GPP standards work is likely to be influenced by two issues in particular: the need to work well with both internet standards and the standards set by particular use cases or verticals.
S0704 The first is perhaps obvious, given the growing significance of mobile phones in internet access, but the second will equally be important in getting the full benefits of 5G.
S0705 The full significance of these drivers will ultimately depend on the market for 5G services.
S0706 People will expect what is delivered over fixed broadband also to be available via 5G connectivity.
S0707 The growing importance of internet data in mobile communications has to some extent been reflected in current 3GPP standards, but many devices also incorporate features based on non-3GPP standards, such as Wi-Fi in mobile phones.
S0708 These wider ICT technologies will need to work in an integrated way with the 5G environment – whether or not they are formally part of 5G.
S0709 5G devices will incorporate not only internet and web standards from Internet Engineering Task Force and W3C but also others (particularly from verticals) that overlap with 5G or add additional linked features.
S0710 These complex interoperability requirements will be defined over time by the market, but meeting them will require a new level of sophistication in the relationship between standards.
S0711 Potential use cases of 5G in sectors such as health or transport have well established standards and ways of working and standards used in these environments will need to integrate with 5G standards.
S0712 Therefore, a high level of collaboration will be needed between vertical sectors’ standards bodies and the ICT standards bodies responsible for the central core of 5G standards.
S0713 Timely development of appropriate standards should help to tackle any security or spectrum use problems before 5G devices come to market, and therefore put supported verticals on a firm footing.
S0714 This will support the early success and long-term value of 5G networks.
S0715 We expect a limited range of standards to receive initial focus; providers will roll-out a set of features that are cost effective for them and attractive to users.
S0716 Enhanced broadband is likely to be the early driver, using lower frequencies for good coverall coverage.
S0717 Other features such as low latency are more difficult to deliver and so standards work on these aspects will have less priority as well as slower provision of equipment.
S0720 Intellectual property rights and standards Intellectual Property Rights (IPR), particularly patents and copyright, are particularly important in the ICT standards community.
S0721 Standards-essential IPR in telecommunications area tends to be freely licensable for a fixed fee, on terms that are meant to be fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory (FRAND).
S0722 Standards that are software or IT centric tend to include IPR on a royalty free (RF) basis (i.e. the IPR is made available gratis), or do not allow IPR at all.
S0723 There is a changing economic model around IPR.
S0724 As ICT becomes more software based, we see increasing use of the RF model in technologies that were previously considered as telecommunications.
S0725 The use of intellectual property in standards can be difficult, not least for the generator of IPR – noticeably for SMEs and academics.
S0726 Where IPR is licensed on a FRAND basis then using it on equitable terms can be difficult, especially for SME users.
S0727 Patent pools and patent portfolios, where patent rights are brought together and there is some level of agreement around charging, can be helpful tools in easing the process of using intellectual property.
S0729 SMEs have different involvements in standards work.
S0730 Some are actively involved because their business is generating ideas and getting them used.
S0731 Many just want standards they can use easily.
S0732 Others find it a difficult area to access and influence.
S0736 RF and payment agreements will both have a part to play in licensing 5G patents (so-called Standards Essential Patents).
S0748 We have had a number of representations suggesting the potential for net neutrality regulation to interact with the technical characteristics of 5G services, including network slicing.
S0772 The Government recognises that having access to fibre is a critical limiting factor in the deployment of 5G and will continue to drive the deployment of new fibre networks.
S0941 This will be particularly important for areas of the country where competition driven markets have struggled to provide the necessary mobile infrastructure.
S0942 The Government agrees with the NIC that infrastructure sharing, in compliance with competition rules, can be an effective and economically efficient way of delivering telecommunications infrastructure, especially in areas where it is uneconomic to deploy competing infrastructure networks.
S0944 Mobile networks have already seen consolidation through joint ventures between Vodafone and Telefonica (CTIL) and between EE and Three (MBNL).
S0945 Independent infrastructure providers that lease capacity on masts to several network operators provide a similar benefit.

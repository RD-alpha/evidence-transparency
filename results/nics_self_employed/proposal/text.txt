S0019 Since 2010, increases in the personal allowance have been worth £1,005 to a typical basic rate taxpayer, whether employed or self-employed.
S0047 But over time this gap has reduced.
S0048 1.14 In particular, the government has introduced a new State Pension for people who reach State Pension age on or after 6 April 2016.
S0049 This is more generous for self-employed people than the previous system and gives the self-employed access to the same State Pension as employees. Under the old rules, the most someone who had been self-employed and making Class 2 contributions could be eligible for was the basic State Pension of £119.30 a week (in 2016-17). ** (Reference: 3)
S0050 The self-employed can now build entitlement to the new State Pension, which is worth £155.65 a week in 2016-17.
S0051 So the self-employed can now receive up to £1,800 a year more in State Pension.
S0052 Someone aged 65 would currently need a private pension pot of around £50,000 to get an annuity that pays £1,800 a year.
S0053 1.15 National Insurance determines eligibility to contributory benefits and state-funded statutory payments such as statutory maternity pay.
S0054 The differences in eligibility to these benefits between employed and self-employed are now mainly limited to certain parental benefits.
S0055 Spring Budget 2017 announces that the government will consider whether there is a case for greater parity in parental benefits between the self-employed and the employed.
S0057 The other remaining difference is contribution-based Jobseeker’s Allowance (JSA).
S0058 JSA is designed to support those looking for a job, rather than self-employed individuals who may be temporarily experiencing a shortage of work.
S0059 In common with other individuals who do not satisfy the conditions of contribution-based JSA, the self-employed may be entitled to appropriate income-related support (dependent on their household income level and savings).
S0069 1.20 Budget 2016 announced that Class 2 NICs would be abolished from April 2018.
S0070 The rationale for abolishing Class 2 is twofold.
S0071 First, the government wants to simplify the NICs system for the self-employed, responding to a recommendation from the Office of Tax Simplification. ** (Reference: 6)
S0072 After this change, self-employed individuals will pay only one class of NICs, rather than two separate amounts calculated via different methods.
S0073 Secondly, the changes make the system more progressive.
S0074 Because Class 2 is a fixed cash amount, it is a regressive charge.
S0076 From April 2018, Class 4 will become the replacement for determining entitlement to benefits. 1.22 Budget 2017 announces that to reflect the significant improvement in access for the self- employed to the new State Pension, and alongside these changes to Class 2, the rate of Class 4 NICs will be brought closer to the rate of employee NICs. ** (Reference: 7)
S0077 The current self-employed NICs system of a flat rate of £2.80 a week plus 9% of profits at the main rate will be replaced by a single charge of 10% in 2018, which will rise to 11% in 2019.

S0512 Class 4 National Insurance contributions ( NICs ) – The government has already announced that it will abolish Class 2 NICs – a flat-rate charge on the self-employed – from April 2018.
S0515 To reduce the differential and reflect more equal pension entitlement, the Budget announces that the main rate of Class 4 NICs will increase from 9% to 10% in April 2018, and to 11% in April 2019.
S0516 Taken together with the abolition of Class 2 NICs , this means that only self-employed individuals with profits above £16,250 will have to pay more NICs .
S0517 Alongside Matthew Taylor’s review into employment practices, the government will consider whether there is a case for greater parity in parental benefits between the employed and self-employed.

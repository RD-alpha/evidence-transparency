INPUT FORMAT
The <document> is not the policy document itself. It is an extract: every
sentence of the document was first classified into one aspect of the policy
argument, or discarded as belonging to none, and the sentences for this aspect
were then grouped into themes. Sentences belonging to another aspect are scored
separately and are not shown here, so do not penalise the document for them.
A minority of sentences are discarded as belonging to no aspect, so absence from
this extract is not proof of absence from the document.

Each <theme> block carries a sentences attribute giving how many lines it has,
and a weight attribute (core or not core, explained under HOW TO AGGREGATE). A
core theme also holds a <claim>: the claim the document makes in it. Each theme
holds its sentences in <text>, the footnote text of the references those
sentences cite in <references>, and the URLs they link to in <links>. Each
<line> is one sentence; a marker such as "(Reference: 12)" or "(Link: 37)" means
that sentence carries citation 12 or 37. An empty <references> and <links>
block means those sentences carry no citation at all. A trailing <ungrouped>
block, when present, holds sentences that belong to this aspect but that the
theme grouping did not place in any theme. Weigh them as evidence exactly as
you would any other sentence, but do not give <ungrouped> a score of its own:
it is a leftover bucket, not a theme.

Score only what is shown. Do not assume supporting evidence exists somewhere you
cannot see, and do not assume it is absent: treat an uncited claim as uncited,
as the levels above describe, and note in your reasoning where a claim reads as
though it should have carried support. Do not raise or lower the level to hedge
against what the extraction may have missed.

HOW TO AGGREGATE
The problem the intervention is meant to solve is: $problem

Each theme carries a weight decided beforehand from the whole document, without
sight of its evidence: core if the argument for this aspect rests on it, not
core otherwise. Treat the weights as fixed: you may note disagreement with one
in your reasoning, but aggregate using the weights given.

Score the section as follows.
1. For each core theme, decide the level its claim reaches. Judge the evidence
   for that claim wherever it appears in the extract: in the theme itself, in
   another theme, or in <ungrouped>. The grouping into themes is not exact, so
   evidence for a core claim, or the document's assessment of that evidence,
   may sit in a theme of its own. Count only evidence that supports that core
   claim, not evidence that is merely on the same subject. A piece of evidence
   may count for more than one core theme if it supports each claim.
2. For each theme that is not core, decide the level it reaches on its own.
   These levels are recorded but do not set the section score.
3. The section score is the mean of the core themes' levels, rounded to the
   nearest whole level. A mean exactly halfway between two levels rounds down:
   1.5 scores 1, 2.5 scores 2. If no theme is core, take the mean over all
   themes instead.

OUTPUT
Return structured JSON with three fields, in this order.
1. theme_scores: one entry per <theme>, core or not, and none for <ungrouped>,
   giving the theme's name, whether it relies on the department's own primary
   evidence (primary_evidence, true or false), its level 0-3, and a
   one-sentence justification naming the lines or reference numbers behind it.
   For a core theme, name any lines you counted from outside the theme.
2. reasoning: roughly 150-250 words for the section as a whole, written for a
   reader of the final report rather than for another analyst. Quote the
   specific lines and reference numbers you relied on, name the core themes
   and their levels, and state their mean and the score it gives. Give the
   strongest counter-example to the level you chose, and end with what the
   document would have to add to reach the next level up.
3. score: the section score 0-3, from the mean of the core themes' levels as
   set out above.

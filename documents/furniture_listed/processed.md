Review of the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire)(Safety) Regulations (FFRs)
S0009 The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations set fire safety requirements for upholstered furniture sold in the UK.
S0010 A review of the Regulations has been ongoing for some time and a consultation was held in 2014 on proposals relating to the FFRs testing regime.
S0011 In light of the feedback from that consultation, the Government published a formal response in March 2015 which explained that we would undertake further work on some of the technical aspects of the proposals, and consider these as part of the wider review which also covered scope and traceability.
S0012 This consultation therefore builds on that work and sets out some revised proposals which include changes to testing requirements, the scope of the regulations and the traceability of products.
S0013 To implement the changes, we propose to make the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire Safety) Regulations 2016, revoking and replacing the existing 1988 Regulations.
S0014 A draft of the 2016 Regulations is at Annex 7.
S0015 In the consultation we are asking for comments on the contents of both the proposals and the draft regulations.
S0016 In addition, we are seeking input on the impacts of these Regulations to ensure our Impact Assessment correctly reflects the likely costs and benefits.

1. Executive summary
S0023 1.
S0024 This consultation seeks your views on proposed changes to the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988.
S0025 The Regulations cover the whole of the UK and set fire safety requirements for a range of upholstered furniture items intended for use in the home, including (but not limited to) sofas, armchairs, beds, divans, sofa-beds, children’s furniture, pushchairs, prams, cots, mattresses, pillows and cushions.
S0026 2. We are proposing these changes because stakeholders have asked us to revise the regulations as they no longer reflect changing consumer expectations and modern furniture manufacturing practices.
S0027 We particularly want to make changes to the testing methods in the regulations so that they better reflect the way current furniture is made and also to allow manufacturers to reduce flame retardant use.
S0028 3.
S0029 Whilst the current FFRs do not stipulate the use of flame retardants, their use is widespread as the primary means of meeting the requirements of the Regulations.
S0030 There are some concerns over the health and environmental impacts of certain FRs: evidence suggests that flame retardant chemicals, particularly brominated flame retardants, when broken down into individual constituents, can be harmful to human and animal health, and the environment.
S0031 Regulatory change therefore has the potential to reduce the use of flame retardants in the production of furniture and bring associated benefits to industry and to consumers.
S0032 4.
S0033 The FFRs are national legislation and unlike most other product safety legislation, they are specific to the UK, and not based on EU harmonised standards.”
S0034 In respect of the UK’s withdrawal from the EU, the Government will be engaging with businesses and key stakeholders up and down the country.
S0035 We will listen to their concerns, aim to limit uncertainty in the transition and ensure our new relationship with the EU works for business.
S0036 5.
S0037 The proposals in this consultation stem from an extensive review process involving a wide range of stakeholders, and also build on a previous consultation on certain aspects of the Regulations in 2014.
S0038 6.
S0039 The proposals include:
S0040 • Scope – A revised definition of furniture and specific list of exclusions; various clarifications on what is in scope of the Regulations; the removal of certain childcare products from scope;
S0041 • Testing – A revised match test for covers (building on previous proposals); anew match test for components to be used in certain circumstances; removal of the cigarette test for most covers;
S0042 • Traceability and enforcement – A new requirement for manufacturers to hold a technical file; a revised approach to the permanent label including the introduction of information on the use of flame retardants; the removal of the requirement for temporary display labels; more time for Trading Standards to bring a prosecution.

5. The proposals
S0093 Introduction
S0094 The existing Regulations
S0095 19.
S0096 The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 (FFRs) have now been in existence for nearly 30 years.
S0097 At the time they were introduced, domestic upholstered furniture presented a very significant threat to safety in the home.
S0098 The natural, fire-resistant materials used in furniture making for centuries, such as wool, cotton and horsehair, had been replaced during the second half of the twentieth century with cheaper foam fillings, making new furniture affordable for all.
S0099 Unfortunately, these new man-made materials were extremely flammable and the result was a sharp increase in fatalities resulting from house fires.
S0100 The Regulations strengthened existing requirements for cover fabrics to be resistant to ignition and introduced a new flammability requirement for foam fillings.
S0101 As a result, the number of house fires and fatalities due to combustible furniture began to fall rapidly.
S0102 20.
S0103 Research commissioned by BIS, and carried out by Greenstreet Berman, shows that fires involving furniture and furnishings increased by about 8% between 1981and 1987. ** (Reference: 1)
S0104 The introduction of the FFRs in 1988 saw this increase level off, and by 2007 the number of fires had fallen by about 38% from the 1987 level.
S0105 Whilst the statistics since 2009 are not directly comparable due to a change in the way information was recorded by the fire services, these have also shown a fall (of around 45% between 2009/10 and 2013/14).
S0106 21.
S0107 The report suggested that, even when community fire safety initiatives (such as the Home Fire Risk Check system introduced in 2004) are taken into the consideration, the FFRs have played an important role in the prevention of house fires and concomitant injury and death.
S0108 However the review undertaken with stakeholders over the past few years has indicated that the Regulations are no longer entirely appropriate to changing consumer expectations, and furniture manufacturing practices.
S0109 Areas of concern include the scope of the Regulations, their enforceability, and the effectiveness of the testing regime.
S0110 In addition, it was felt that the testing regime, most particularly the match test prescribed in the FFRs, could be updated so that it reflects more closely the way modern furniture is constructed.
S0111 Flame retardants
S0112 22.
S0113 Evidence suggests that flame retardant chemicals, particularly brominated flame retardants, when broken down into individual constituents, can be harmful to human and animal health, and the environment.
S0114 (Research references at Annex 5).
S0115 For this reason, REACH the EU’s chemical legislation, is proposing to restrict the use of decaBDE, a Brominated Flame Retardant (BFR) widely used in furniture production. ** (Reference: 2)
S0116 If adopted the restriction would come into force in 2018.
S0117 A substitute has been found for decaBDE but the possibility of it also being restricted at some point in the future cannot be ruled out.
S0118 The necessity of changing chemicals to comply with REACH
S0119 puts a financial burden on the furniture industry which could be avoided if chemical use were reduced.
S0120 The requirements of the Stockholm Convention are also significant.
S0121 The Convention, ratified by 179 countries including the UK, obliges these countries to reduce and eliminate the production, use and release into the environment of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) in order to protect human health and the environment.
S0122 23.
S0123 Whilst the current FFRs do not stipulate the use of flame retardants, their use is widespread as the primary means of meeting the requirements of the Regulations.
S0124 Regulatory change therefore has the potential to reduce the use of flame retardants, and reduce the burden on industry.
S0125 24.
S0126 All of this also needs to be considered in a wider global context.
S0127 Safety standards for UK furniture are acknowledged as being the highest in Europe.
S0128 Unlike most other product safety legislation, they are specific to the UK, and not based on EU harmonised standards.
S0129 Some European Member States object strongly to the use of flame retardant chemicals in furniture because of the growing evidence of their deleterious effect on health and the environment, and EU consumers are deterred from purchasing UK products.
S0130 25.
S0131 Concerns about the safety of flame retardant chemicals are not confined to Europe.
S0132 Lobbying by environmental bodies, combined with consumer concern, has resulted in a change in the furniture flammability standard in California to exclude the use of flame retardants, and Washington banning five flame retardants used in furniture and children’s products. ** (Reference: 3)
S0133 Reducing UK manufacturers’ use of flame retardants should therefore also make it easier for them to export to Europe and beyond.
S0134 This consultation
S0135 26.
S0136 In this context, and building on the review mentioned previously, BIS consulted in 2014 on a number of proposed changes to the FFRs testing regime (specifically the match and cigarette tests).
S0137 However, the match test is only part of the story: clear feedback was received from stakeholders participating in the 2014 consultation that it would be preferable to see all possible changes set out together and in its formal response to the consultation in March 2015, the Government set out its intention to bring all the changes together as part of the wider review.
S0138 This message was also reflected in subsequent discussions in two stakeholder workshops held in the summer of 2015, where these potential wider changes were discussed in more detail.
S0139 27.
S0140 Building on stakeholder feedback, therefore, this consultation brings together proposals on three main elements of the Regulations:
S0141 • Scope
S0142 • Testing
S0143 • Traceability and enforcement

S0145 BEIS is asking stakeholders to consider the overall effect of the proposed changes and to respond with opinions on their possible individual and collective impacts.
S0146 We believe this will result in a more balanced assessment of the potential effects of the revised regulations than was possible with the previous consultation and also reflect ideas put forward at the 2015 workshops.

S0150 Scope
S0151 Definition
S0152 30.
S0153 The scope of the 1988 Regulations is presently defined by:
S0154 • A list of what is included
S0155 • A list of exclusions
S0156 31.
S0157 However, tastes and fashions in furniture have changed over the past 30 years.
S0158 There is now a lack of clarity as to what now falls within the scope of the Regulations.
S0159 Following discussions at the stakeholder workshops organised by BIS in July and August 2015, it was generally agreed that a more holistic, ‘everything is included unless it’s specifically excluded’ approach, which would require a much reduced supplementary list of exclusions, would be simpler and more easily understood by those in the production chain, retailers and enforcement agencies.
S0160 32.
S0161 Based on discussions at the workshops, we therefore propose the following wording which we believe to be comprehensive and to fulfil the criteria outlined above:
S0162 These regulations cover any item of domestic furniture which is ordinarily intended for private use in a dwelling and comprises a cover fabric and a filling.
S0163 33.
S0164 Our intention is that unless a product which meets this definition is specifically excluded, it will be within scope of the revised Regulations.
S0165 Proposal: A single definition to cover all items in scope, supplemented by a list of exclusions.
S0166 Clarifications and exclusions
S0167 34.
S0168 The 1988 Regulations have a number of ‘grey areas’ including scatter cushions, seat pads, sleeping bags and washable mattress protectors.
S0169 We want to set out a definitive exclusions list to clarify the Regulations whilst generally seeking to maintain the current status quo.
S0170 The definitive removal from the Regulations’ scope of ambiguous products should reduce the number of products which need to be tested, with a consequent reduction in the amount of flame retardant chemicals in the home.
S0171 These products would of course continue to be covered by the General Product Safety Regulations (GPSR) and any relevant British or European Standards.
S0172 35.
S0173 Products for possible exclusion were discussed at the 2015 workshops.
S0174 In particular, some stakeholders argued strongly for the exclusion of certain baby products predominantly used outside the home and this is discussed further below.
S0175 We also set out our proposals for the application of the Regulations to second-hand furniture, together with consideration of other ‘grey areas’ including outdoor furniture.
S0176 Sleeping bags and mattress protectors
S0177 36.
S0178 Our proposal is that sleeping bags and washable mattress protectors which can be put in a washing machine should be excluded because, in the course of the product’s lifetime, any flame retardant chemicals are soon washed off, rendering flame retardant treatment pointless.
S0179 This is the same rationale currently used for duvets.
S0180 Proposal: Sleeping bags and mattress protectors which can be put in a washing machine to be excluded.
S0181 Cushions and seat pads
S0182 37.
S0183 A scatter cushion is usually defined as a small cushion which can be moved to any position in a room and is supplied separately; that is, it does not comprise an integral part of an item of furniture.
S0184 In the same way, a seat pad is generally accepted as being a small cushion intended to be used with, for example, a wooden kitchen or dining chair.
S0185 38.
S0186 The 1988 Regulations require that only the filling materials for such items must satisfy the relevant ignition test and this will continue to be the case in the revised Regulations.
S0187 The 1988 Regulations do not offer a definition of either item, but it is commonly accepted that a scatter cushion has dimensions of less than 60cm x 60cmx normal product thickness.
S0188 A seat pad is accepted as having dimensions no bigger than 30cm x 30cm x 1cm thick.
S0189 Our proposal is that the revised Regulations will formalise these definitions.
S0190 Proposal: Scatter cushions and seat pads to remain excluded from cover tests but the definition of these products to be specified more clearly.
S0191 Outdoor furniture
S0192 39.
S0193 Currently, the Regulations apply to outdoor furniture which is suitable for use in dwellings, but do not apply to garden furniture if it is (a) not suitable for use in dwellings (e.g., if it could not physically be brought indoors), (b) is non-upholstered (e.g., deck chairs), or (c) consists only of cover fabric (e.g., sunshades or canopies).
S0194 40.
S0195 However, in practice the treatment of outdoor furniture varies geographically and between manufacturers, leading to inconsistency and confusion.
S0196 In an effort to resolve this, and following discussions at the 2015 stakeholder workshops, BEIS is proposing that outdoor furniture be excluded from the Regulations if (a) it is not suitable for use inside the home; and (b) it is clearly labelled to demonstrate that it is for outdoor use only as it does not comply with the Regulations.
S0197 The Regulations themselves would remain high level, but we would expect the finer details of this proposal to be agreed between industry and Trading Standards to ensure consistency.
S0198 Proposal: Outdoor furniture unsuitable for use inside the home, and clearly labelled as not complying with the Regulations, to be out of scope.
S0199 Baby and child products
S0200 41.
S0201 Currently, the Regulations apply to a range of baby products, including pushchairs, prams and carry cots.
S0202 However, there is a good argument that inclusion of these products does not actually contribute to the wider objectives of the Regulations – namely reduction in the risk of serious injuries or loss of life due to house fires – for two reasons.
S0203 42.
S0204 Firstly, many of the products in question do not pose the same risks as upholstered furniture because of the way they are used.
S0205 For example, prams and pushchairs are predominantly used (and intended to be used) outside the home, and are only brought inside on an exceptional basis.
S0206 Moreover, even when inside the home, they would not be exposed to the same risks of accidental ignition (for example from smoking materials).
S0207 43.
S0208 Secondly, even if ignited, the products contain a much lower fuel load than other upholstered items, and therefore any fire would be less likely to spread.
S0209 44.
S0210 As well as the risk of house fires being low from these items, the risk of fire-related injury to the child themselves would also be low since many of the products in question are not intended to be used with the child left unattended.
S0211 Moreover, these products are already covered by the UK’s General Product Safety Regulations and robust standards for childcare products, which include a requirement for fire resistance.
S0212 45.
S0213 We are therefore proposing to remove from the scope of the Regulations those childcare items covered by standards BS EN1888 (wheeled child conveyances – pushchairs, prams, etc.) and BS EN1466 (carry cots and stands).
S0214 Childcare articles which have more in common with mattresses (for example padded playpens) would be subject to the same requirements as mattresses (i.e. filling test only), while other children’s furniture such as highchairs would continue to be within scope of the Regulations as a whole, subject to any other relevant exclusions.
S0215 46.
S0216 As shown in the Impact Assessment, this could bring significant savings by reducing testing costs and administrative burdens for manufacturers of these items, without undermining the objective of the legislation.
S0217 Moreover, this could help to address particular concerns raised about the use of flame retardants in children’s products.
S0218 Proposal: Childcare articles covered by BS EN1888 (wheeled child conveyances) and BS EN1466 (carry cots and stands) to be removed from scope.
S0219 Padded playpens to be treated in the same way as mattresses.
S0220 Second-hand furniture
S0221 47.
S0222 Second-hand furniture produced after 1950 is currently within scope of the 1988 Regulations and subject to the same requirements as new furniture.
S0223 However, in practical terms, this is enforced by ensuring that furniture offered for sale as second-
S0224 hand, or second-hand furniture donated to charity, bears the original permanent label.
S0225 48.
S0226 We propose that the revised Regulations reaffirm this existing practice by making it a requirement for second-hand furniture to bear the relevant permanent label which would have been applicable at the time when the product was first supplied (i.e. the ‘current’ label for furniture originally supplied prior to the new Regulations coming into force, or the ‘new’ label for furniture originally supplied after that date).
S0227 In addition, second hand products would continue to be subject to the requirements of the General Product Safety Regulations.
S0228 Pre-1950 furniture would continue to be exempt from the Regulations.
S0229 Proposal: Second-hand furniture offered for sale, or donated to charity, should bear the original permanent label.

S0274 Objectives

S0318 Whilst keeping the test method the same as in the current Regulations (BS 5852:Part1:1979), it also proposed two options for test structure, one where the cover would be placed directly over the foam (Filling 1), and one where the cover would be tested over foam together with a fibre wrap (Filling 2).

S0320 **(Link: 16)
S0321 59.
S0322 Filling 1 was included to provide a simpler specification where covers are placed directly over the foam filling in the final product.
S0323 60.
S0324 The chemical specification of the foam was one area which attracted feedback.
S0325 However, adverse comments focused mainly on the use of non-combustion modified foam in the Filling 1 scenario.

S0330 We have also considered two subsidiary questions in relation to Filling 2:
S0331 62.
S0332 Should there be a chemical specification for the combustion-modified foam?
S0333 Feedback received via the BSI process suggested that expert agreement on a particular foam would not be possible; and that if the Filling 1 route was not adopted, the chemical specification of the foam would be less of an issue.
S0334 This was also confirmed in our technical panel discussions.
S0335 We do not therefore believe a chemical specification for the foam is needed.
S0336 63.
S0337 Should there be further specification of the fibre-wrap?
S0338 We are proposing to specify the fibre wrap according to the material and its width (see draft Regulations for further detail).
S0339 However, discussions in the technical panel highlighted that it might also be helpful to define a range for the density of the fibre, to avoid extremes which could unduly influence the outcome of the test.
S0340 We will therefore work with fibre manufacturers during the consultation period to identify a range for fibre density which reflects the fibre commonly used in furniture construction.
S0341 Proposal: Match test for all covers to be conducted over a single test structure – CM foam and fibre wrap, as specified in the draft Regulations
S0342 Protective covers and ‘hole formation’
S0343 64.
S0344 The current regulations work on the premise that the cover for furniture should be ignition resistant and also serve to protect the upholstery contents (the filling).
S0345 As well as seeking a more representative format for the test structure, the 2014 proposal therefore explicitly separated out these two functions and required all covers to be tested for hole formation as an indication of whether they would meet the protective criterion as well as the ignitability requirement.
S0346 Where covers failed to meet the protective requirement, BIS therefore originally proposed that they would be classed as “not protective” and industry would be required to ensure the fire safety of components close to the cover through a new match test for components.
S0347 65.
S0348 Feedback on this proposal raised a number of issues including:
S0349 • whether defining protection by “hole formation” was workable (particular with respect to measurement and repeatability);
S0350 • whether the additional testing for hole formation was proportionate – given that most types of cover were likely to be non-protective; and
S0351 • how this would affect business supply chains.
S0352 66.
S0353 Some comments from the BSI process suggested that the requirement should be dropped entirely.
S0354 However, doing this would mean assuming that all covers that passed the ignition test were not protective – which could cause difficulties in certain sectors and unnecessarily restricts industry’s options.
S0355 67.
S0356 We have therefore worked with our technical panel to develop a protective cover option so that those who wish to use a protective cover route to compliance can do so, thereby avoiding the need to test components close to the cover.
S0357 We have considered a range of options, including the use of a chemical ‘marker’ on the test foam to indicate flame penetration, or using non-CM foam and seeing whether it ignites.
S0358 However, whilst these options have certain advantages, they also have their own drawbacks.
S0359 We have therefore chosen to propose a methodology based on the previous ‘hole formation’ idea.
S0360 68.
S0361 Building on the 2014 proposal, we propose that a cover is defined as ‘protective’ if, when tested under our revised match test (either on its own or in combination with any liner or non-flame retardant treated fire barrier that will appear in the final product), it does not form a visible hole of more than 2mm.
S0362 69.
S0363 Whilst we acknowledge this could be burdensome for the manufacturer, we would only expect this approach to be used in a minority of cases.
S0364 Moreover, if a cover is shown to be protective, the manufacturer would not need to incur the cost of testing their components close to the cover.
S0365 Proposal: A cover is defined as ‘protective’ if, when tested under our revised match test (either on its own or in combination with any liner or non-flame retardant treated fire barrier that will appear in the final product), it does not form a visible hole more than 2mm.
S0366 Components close to the cover
S0367 70.
S0368 As noted above, we understand that some manufacturers will not want to go down the route of ensuring their components are match-resistant, hence the development of the ‘protective cover’ option.
S0369 Alternatively, under our proposals manufacturers can continue to deploy a Schedule 3 interliner, which would also exempt them from the need to test components (see below).
S0370 However, if neither of these approaches are used, there will be a requirement to ensure that components close to the cover – defined as those within (inside) 40mm of the visible cover – are tested for fire safety, using a new ‘match test for components’.
S0371 71.
S0372 The match test for components was a part of the 2014 proposal, alongside a suggestion that there should be a list of exempt materials.
S0373 The previous proposal also introduced the idea that components used close to the cover could themselves provide a protective function (the logic being that if the cover itself was not protective, then a protective material/ component close to the cover that was laid over other, flammable components in the filling would perform the necessary barrier function.)
S0374 72.
S0375 Although the aim of an “exclusion” list was to make management of the components easier, it is clear that legally, an exclusion list could only be relied on if the list were part of the Regulations.
S0376 Feedback in consultation however suggested that since upholstery design changes continually, the list would have to be updated annually – which is not feasible.
S0377 In addition, there were doubts in some quarters as to the benefits of dealing with a list, as opposed to managing without.
S0378 It was also not clear that the availability of a list would make enforcement any easier (especially once our traceability proposals are taken into account).
S0379 73.
S0380 In terms of the concept of ‘protective components’, feedback suggests that (a) there are unlikely to be many, if any, materials currently used which would perform this function; and (b) the idea of protective components adds complexity for both manufacturers and enforcers.
S0381 74.
S0382 In this consultation, we are therefore proposing that we drop the suggestion of an externally managed list of exemptions.
S0383 This means that where a non-protective cover is used, the manufacturer will need to ensure that all components close to the cover are compliant with the match test for components.
S0384 We recognise that this may mean some re-sourcing of flammable materials currently used within fillings.
S0385 However, while some feedback suggests this will be difficult, other feedback suggests that, for the majority of components, there are alternative materials that could be found and that re-sourcing would not therefore be problematic, at least in the medium-long term.
S0386 75.
S0387 For the avoidance of doubt, components close to the cover are defined as all materials (excluding the filling, which is subject to separate requirements, but including any lining materials not tested in combination with the cover) within (i.e. inside) 40mm of the visible cover and in the filling.
S0388 The 40mm measurement reflects the likely depth of flame penetration.
S0389 The match test for components is set out in Schedule 5 of the new draft Regulations.
S0390 Proposal: if neither a protective cover, nor a Schedule 3 interliner, is used in the final product, then all components close the cover must pass the new ‘match test for components’.
S0391 This includes any lining materials not already tested in combination with the cover.
S0392 Innovation and flexibility
S0393 76.
S0394 In presenting these amendments to the 2014 proposal, we have tried to improve our recognition of the way business currently operates and to give industry players more choice as to how they prefer to adapt (for example in dividing the new responsibilities for testing).
S0395 We also want to encourage innovation, not just in development of fire barrier technology, but also in how furniture filling is managed and arranged.
S0396 77.
S0397 We recognise, however, that some sections of the market – including some re- upholsters – currently use Schedule 3 interliners to meet the requirements of the Regulations and will want to continue to do so, for the sake of least disrupting their existing business.
S0398 It is also possible (though costly) that certain players will want to use a Schedule 3 interliner in combination with a non-protective match test cover to avoid the need to test components close to the cover.
S0399 Our proposals therefore also allow for this approach as a route to compliance.
S0400 Proposal: Schedule 3 interliners can continue to be used as a means to comply with the Regulations.
S0401 Where a Schedule 3 interliner is used, components close to the cover would not need to pass the modified match test.
S0402 Cigarette test
S0403 78.
S0404 In the original proposal, BIS suggested that it would be possible to remove the cigarette test for any fabric that passed the match test, on the grounds that experience in test houses over the years had shown that in nearly all cases, fabrics that passed the match test also passed the cigarette case.
S0405 79.
S0406 The responses gathered through the BSI process, and subsequent discussions with our technical panel, confirmed that in 99% of past cases, the correlation between match test and cigarette test pass clearly existed.
S0407 Whilst it was noted that for some leathers and non-synthetic fabrics this relationship did not hold, we believe that the proportionate approach is still to remove the requirement to test against the cigarette test for those materials which are required to pass the match test.
S0408 80.
S0409 This would, of course, mean the cigarette test still had to be passed for fabrics that are not tested under the match test (in accordance with Schedule 3 of the current Regulations).
S0410 We are aware that there are technical difficulties with the current cigarette test, in that it is hard for test houses to procure the specified cigarette for testing.
S0411 However, we do not propose to resolve this issue here.
S0412 Finding an appropriate alternative (for example a heated coil) could be something for industry to consider with BSI.

S0452 Traceability and enforcement
S0453 83.
S0454 The 2015 stakeholder workshops revealed both a desire on the part of Trading Standards for clearer supply chain information and a willingness on the part of manufacturers and retailers to provide such information, as far as they are able to do so.
S0455 We believe that specifying the information and traceability requirements more clearly will help ensure product safety for consumers and aid Trading Standards by enabling enforcement officers to trace a non-compliant product back to its place of manufacture, via importers, distributors and wholesalers.
S0456 Accurate record-keeping throughout the supply chain will mean that responsibility for safe products is shared equally between all those involved.
S0457 84.
S0458 The traceability proposals set out below reflect existing good practice within the sector and are based on those for other products thereby bringing furniture into line with other product sectors, for example toys and electrical goods.
S0459 As a result of discussions with stakeholders, BEIS is proposing that manufacturers (and importers where applicable) must keep product records, known as technical files, for 10 years dating from when the product is first placed on the market.
S0460 Technical files must be
S0461 made available upon request to enforcement authorities.
S0462 A technical file is essentially required to facilitate compliance and enforcement, and to show how the product meets the requirements of the Regulations.
S0463 We therefore propose that the technical file should include, as a minimum:
S0464 • the (a) name, (b) registered trade name or registered trade mark, and (c) contact address of the manufacturer or importer who first supplied the article in the UK;
S0465 • the date on which the article was manufactured or imported (in its finished form)into the United Kingdom;
S0466 • the type, batch, serial or model number, or other element, to enable identification of the product in line with the permanent label;
S0467 • a description of all the (a) filling materials; (b) covering materials; and (c) components (identifying those within 40mm of the cover where relevant) included in the article;
S0468 • a description of the flame retardants included in the cover, where applicable;
S0469 • evidence, including the details and results of any test carried out on the furniture in question or any of its components, to demonstrate that the product is compliant with the Regulations.
S0470 85.
S0471 In addition, and as for the current Regulations, manufacturers will also be required to ensure each product carries a permanent label.
S0472 However, unlike the current Regulations, we propose that there should be no distinction between a ‘short’ and ‘long’ label, and that the label should focus on the minimum information needed to ensure traceability of the product, together with some limited information for the consumer.
S0473 We therefore propose that the label should include:
S0474 • the words “CARELESSNESS CAUSES FIRE” and “DO NOT REMOVE THISLABEL”;
S0475 • the (a) name, (b) registered trade name or registered trade mark, and (c) contact address of the manufacturer;
S0476 • the (a) name, (b) registered trade name or registered trade mark, and (c) contact address of the importer (where applicable);
S0477 • a type, batch, serial or model number, or other element, to enable identification of the product;
S0478 • a declaration that the article complies with the requirements of the Regulations(and a statement, where applicable, that it does so via the use of a Schedule 3 interliner);
S0479 • an indication (by text, symbol or other means) of whether flame retardants have been used to ensure the cover meets the requirements of the Regulations.
S0480 86.
S0481 Our proposal for the inclusion of information on flame retardants on the permanent label is in response to public concerns about these chemicals which were articulated
S0482 at the stakeholder workshops, and in subsequent meetings, by a group of concerned charities.
S0483 We believe the inclusion of this information is important in helping to inform consumer choice in the purchase of furniture intended for long-term use in the home.
S0484 The other information specified above is all included to aid enforcement agencies and thereby improve product safety.
S0485 Retailers (distributors) must also continue to ensure that the permanent label is present before offering the item for sale.
S0486 Proposal: Manufacturers (and importers where applicable) must keep technical files.
S0487 Manufacturers must ensure each item of furniture carries a permanent label to enable traceability, and others in the supply chain must ensure the permanent label is present.
S0488 Proposal: The permanent label should provide relevant information to consumers on the use of flame retardants in the product, with further detail provided in the technical file.
S0489 87.
S0490 Finally, we propose removing the requirement for additional display labels, since in practice these are immediately removed by the consumer after purchase and are unlikely to affect consumer behaviour with respect to safety.
S0491 Proposal: The existing requirement for (non-permanent) display labels is removed for all products.
S0492 88.
S0493 BEIS is confident that, overall, the traceability requirements set out above will not place a significant additional burden on business as, at the workshops, it was agreed that it already standard practice for reputable retailers to supply much of the information that will be required.
S0494 The proposal will therefore transform current good practice into legislation in order to encompass those suppliers and manufacturers whose procedures are less robust.
S0495 89.
S0496 More widely, we believe all the changes set out above will assist Trading Standards in enforcing the Regulations.
S0497 However, to assist them in taking enforcement action where businesses do not comply, we are proposing to extend the time available to bring a prosecution under the Regulations from 6 months to 12 months, in line with other consumer safety legislation.
S0498 Proposal: Time for Trading Standards to carry out a prosecution is extended from 6 months to 12 months.

S0519 Transition period
S0520 91.
S0521 We recognise that change can be costly, and that businesses need time to make the necessary changes in line with supply chain pressures.
S0522 In the 2014 consultation on changes to the testing regime, we proposed an 18 month transition period.
S0523 However, concern was expressed at the stakeholder workshops about re-testing costs and loss of stock, particularly for SMEs.
S0524 92.

S0527 Proposal: The Regulations include a transition period of 24 months

S0532 We would not envisage the first review to be a substantial exercise as we are unlikely to have a significant body of evidence on the operation of the new Regulations, but this will provide an opportunity to reflect on the practical application of the legislation.

S1106 1. Background to the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations
S1107 1.1 The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 (FFRs) provide flammability performance levels for all UK domestic upholstered furniture, e.g. sofas, chairs, cushions, pillows, mattress fillings, etc.
S1108 While the FFRs do not stipulate any particular route to compliance, in practice manufacturers mostly choose to use Flame Retardants (FRs) as the most cost-effective solution.
S1109 1.2 The FFRs are successful in preventing injury and loss of life.
S1110 A BIS-commissioned report in 2009 shows that the current regulations were annually saving around 54 lives, preventing around 800 injuries and over 1,000 fires.
S1111 These savings to health and property were valued at around £140m per year. Therefore, any changes to the regulation have the primary objective of consumer protection and safety. ** (Reference: 4)
S1112 1.3 Enforcement of the FFRs is the responsibility of Trading Standards, with powers derived from the primary legislation, the Consumer Protection Act 1987.
S1113 Regular exercises by Trading Standards reveal that a constant threat to UK consumers is from non-compliant furniture imports, because most of the rest of the world provides little flammability protection for upholstered products.
S1114 2.
S1115 What is the problem?
S1116 Rationale for intervention
S1117 Testing regime
S1118 2.1 The FFRs have not been updated, aside from minor amendments, since 1988 and stakeholders, whilst strongly supportive of the FFRs, have often lobbied for an update.
S1119 One of the fire resistance tests, the match test, no longer reflects the way furniture is now manufactured.
S1120 For the past four years, BIS has been working closely with all key stakeholders on a review of the FFRs to update the testing requirements and provide a way to reduce the use of FRs.
S1121 2.2 There is growing body of literature that has linked health and environmental harm with the flame retardant chemicals (brominated, chlorinated and phosphate) used in furniture. ** (Reference: 5)
S1122 Furniture flame retardants can be associated with endocrine disruption, immunotoxicity, cancer, and/or reproductive and neurological impairments, lowered IQ, and hyperactivity.
S1123 Flame retardants migrate out of furniture, settle in dust, and are ingested by humans and animals.
S1124 Young children have the highest blood levels due to hand-to-mouth behaviour.
S1125 In the USA, a majority of residential fire deaths result from inhalation of toxic gases, and soot and smoke can obscure escape.
S1126 One study indicates that US fire fighters have high rates of types of cancers associated with dioxin exposure; the dioxins produced when flame retardants burn are believed to contribute.
S1127 There are some signs in the UK (from press and consumers) that consumer concern over the chemical treatment of furniture is growing here.
S1128 2.3
S1129 The current flammability tests required by the FFRs are: match and cigarette tests for cover fabrics and the 'crib 5' test for filling materials.
S1130 Some FRs are needed for filling materials; however, these are largely non-contentious.
S1131 More potentially harmful FRs - particularly the brominated variety (BFRs) - are used on cover fabrics to meet the stringent requirements of the match test.
S1132 These are applied either by impregnating the fabric or by 'backcoating' it.
S1133 There is growing evidence that a) BFRs are worn away during normal use, getting into house dust and b) they damage the environment by releasing toxins and dioxins when burnt or dumped in landfill at end of life (see Annex 1 for evidence linking chemicals with environmental and health outcomes).
S1134 2.4 The current FFRs match test (see text box) requires cover fabrics to be tested over highly- flammable (and now illegal in the UK) polyurethane foam fillings.
S1135 This means the additional flame
S1136 resistance supplied by the combustion-modified fillings that are present in the final product is not utilised, leading to a higher than necessary ignition resistance standard in the cover fabrics (and more chemicals used to meet it).
S1137 More information is given in Annex 2 on the details of the test changes.
S1138 Box 1: The match test •
S1139 The current match test under the FFRs requires cover fabric to be tested over highly flammable polyurethane foam.
S1140 However, this foam would not pass the FFRs filling test, i.e. it is not found in furniture sold in the UK.
S1141 The EU match test, by contrast, requires cover fabrics to be tested over the same combustion-modified foam that appears in final products, which is the basis of BIS's new proposal.
S1142 The proposed new match test will be undertaken over representative actual foam fillings.
S1143 This will reduce the amount of flame retardant chemicals needed to pass the match test; without compromising the safety of the final product.
S1144 •
S1145 The current test serves two purposes: to reduce the ignitability of the cover fabric and to protect the filling material underneath.
S1146 The current test does not take account of the fact that the combustion- modified foam present in the actual product reduces the ignitability of the cover fabric and is also, by its nature, more protected than the test PU foam.
S1147 Box 2: The Cigarette test • We also intend to remove the existing cigarette test for materials which are required to pass the match test.
S1148 This is because the cigarette test is less stringent than the match test and many fabrics have never failed the test.
S1149 The main reason they do not fail the cigarette test is because they must also pass the match test, which is a much more severe test.
S1150 2.5 Government intervention is necessary to amend a flammability test requirement that no longer matches current manufacturing methods and has led to furniture producers and retailers using large amounts of flame retardant chemicals to comply with the regulations.
S1151 The new test updates the method to reflect current manufacturing practice and also introduces the potential to reduce the cost of meeting safety requirements to ensure furniture is fire resistant.
S1152 Scope of the regulations
S1153 2.6 Currently the scope of the regulations is defined by a prescriptive list of what’s in and what’s out.
S1154 The 1988 regulations give a list of inclusions and a short list of exclusions but this can give rise to ambiguities – for example, sofas with scatter back cushions (where the sofa itself would be covered by the regulations but the covers of scatter cushions could be considered out of scope).
S1155 We intend to clarify the scope and test potential exclusions (such as some baby products) through consultation.
S1156 Traceability
S1157 2.7 Currently the traceability requirements in the FFRs are limited to information about the product and the testing it has undergone.
S1158 What is lacking is information on the product supply chain which is key to effective traceability to ensure the safety of consumer products, by allowing product recall if a product is faulty and enabling the enforcement authorities to easily identify and locate the source of any problem.
S1159 Accurate record-keeping throughout the manufacturing process would aid traceability, and responsibility for safe products should be shared throughout the supply chain.

S1213 Route  - interliner for cellulosic fabrics
S1214 This route already exists under the current regulations.
S1215 It allows for an interliner to be present in the finished item between the cover fabric and the foam filling.
S1216 All interliners need to meet the requirements set out in ‘Schedule 3’ of the 1988 FFRs.
S1217 Currently if the interliner is made of a ‘relevant’ material (eg is cellulosic as defined in Regulation 8) then the fabric cover does not need to meet the match test requirement.

S1231 The simplified match test allows for a reduction in FR use on cover fabrics.
S1232 Where these fabrics are deemed to be ‘non-protective’ then components close to the cover ie within 40mm must be tested for fire resistance.
S1233 Given current construction methods we expect this to be the most favoured route to compliance for around 50% of products.

S1235 Most fabrics which pass the match test will automatically pass the cigarette test
S1236 because the match test is more severe.

S1239 Scope
S1240 4.6 The key question in relation to scope is whether certain products, or groups of products, should or should not be within the scope of the FFRs.
S1241 There is a debate about certain baby products (specifically prams/pushchairs and child car seats).
S1242 These are currently specifically included in the FFRs, but the Baby Products Association in response argued strongly for their removal as they impose a burden on manufacturers which is disproportionate to the risk (not least because of concerns about the use of FRs in baby products, and the fact that pushchairs and car seats are predominantly for use outside the home and therefore present a lower fire risk).
S1243 We propose to test this approach in consultation bye including an option that these products are removed from scope.
S1244 4.7 In addition, there are a number of ‘grey areas’ in terms of what falls within scope of the current Regulations.
S1245 These include outdoor furniture, scatter cushions, sleeping bags, and washable mattress protectors.
S1246 In general, we would propose that the rules here should be clarified in a way which seeks to maintain the status quo as much as possible.
S1247 The removal of products falling in these ambiguous areas would reduce the number of products which require testing and thereby the use of flame retardants.
S1248 In line with the approach taken in other product safety legislation (the so-called ‘New Legislative Framework’ or NLF)
S1249 we propose to explicitly remove second hand furniture from the flammability requirements of these regulations and their safety will be ensured through the General Product Safety Regulations as for the majority of other consumer products.
S1250 We plan to use the consultation to explore the appetite for removing these products from scope.

S1336 5.11 The transition period chosen can affect the cost to business in two ways.

S1492 Consumer
S1493 5.45 There will be a safeguarding of improved fire safety for consumers as a result of a furniture testing regime that more closely mirrors the reality of furniture manufacture and is easier to enforce.
S1494 The existing FFRs have been successful in preventing injury and loss of life in the UK.
S1495 However, it has become clear that there are weaknesses in the current match and to ensure the continuation of those fire safety benefits of around £140m per year an updated testing regime is required.

S1498 Health benefits from reduced use of FRs
S1499 5.47 Various researchers have shown that brominated flame retardants are present in house dust, from various consumer products such as furniture (see Annex for sources).
S1500 Traces of BFRs have been found in human blood, particularly children, and in pets, i.e. because these tend to be in closer proximity to house dust (see 5.3 and 5.4 above).
S1501 Other research shows long-term effects in rats from inducing BFRs at the post-natal stage, e.g. loss of attention and mood swings (see Annex for sources).
S1502 UK furniture retailers/manufacturers have reported that they are receiving increasing correspondence from the public concerned about the health effects of chemicals in furniture.
S1503 Environmental benefits
S1504 5.48 At present, old upholstered furniture ends up in land-fill.
S1505 Some of the FR chemicals present therein leach out into the environment and, according to Food Standards Agency research, get into the food chain (see Annex for sources). REACH ** (Reference: 14)
S1506 the EU’s chemical legislation, is proposing to restrict the use of decaBDE, a Brominated Flame Retardant (BFR) widely used in furniture production.
S1507 If adopted the restriction would come into force in 2018.
S1508 Defra reports that there are significant problems with the destruction of FR-containing products at end-life, e.g. with building waste.
S1509 BEIS, therefore, wishes to alleviate this problem by introducing an FR-reducing flammability test (as well as allowing for innovation in new 'barrier' technology that could eventually lead to a total absence of FRs in furniture).
S1510 5.49 BEIS acknowledges that in practice manufacturers will continue to use FRs at least in the short term, and that that means products containing them will still need to be disposed of safely at end- life (when forthcoming EU legislation demands it).
S1511 However, the new test will allow the use of inherently flame-resistant fibres which would not pass the current test.
S1512 We believe an increased demand for such fibres will drive down the price so that their use will be cheaper than FR treatment costs.
S1513 There are also other new developing technologies which should also become more attractive to manufacturers and thereby decrease in cost.

S1523 Fire safety
S1524 6.2 BEIS believes the new test will maintain safety, because testing can now include previously unregulated parts of furniture that can be exposed to flame where foam filling is thinner, e.g. the arms.
S1525 Test houses inform us that in recent years, cheap and highly flammable materials have sometimes appeared in these unregulated parts, making the overall product more flammable than was envisaged at the introduction of the FFRs.
S1526 Cost savings of flame retardant treatment not passed onto furniture producers and ultimately the consumers
S1527 6.3 This risk is minimal as furniture manufacturers are well aware of the breakdown of treatment costs, i.e. that half the cost covers the processing while half covers the chemicals, and will therefore be able to negotiate effectively with treatment companies.
S1528 Some larger manufacturers have their own treatment facilities so will directly benefit from the savings.
S1529 7 Micro/SME Business Assessment
S1530 7.1 There are 3,800 SME furniture manufacturers in the UK, comprising roughly 97% of all manufacturers. Micro/SME businesses are not exempted from the requirements of the FFRs because they must produce furniture that is as safe for the consumer as that produced by larger companies. ** (Reference: 15)
S1531 7.2 Due to economies of scale, anecdotal evidence has suggested SMEs can pay three times more than larger companies for FR treatment of their fabrics.
S1532 This doesn’t mean their savings will be 3 times higher, i.e. because the saving is in the cost of chemicals not the treatment process.
S1533 The resource cost saving will be the same with larger companies.
S1534 However, there is an uncertainty as to whether SMEs will receive the cost savings from treatment plants or cover fabric sellers, an issue raised in the 2014 consultation responses which we plan to test in our next consultation.
S1535 7.3 However the costs (outlined above) for the initial transition and ongoing routes to compliance, may be disproportionately larger for SMEs eg they may be less likely to already hold the technical information required and the costs of compliance may bear more heavily on small firms.
S1536 We plan to test this in consultation.
S1537 We will implement in a way to reduce the costs to small businesses as far as possible eg we have extended the transition period to 24 months and will prepare clear guidance.
S1538 8 Costs and benefits for Option 3 - revoke the regulations and rely on EU standards
S1539 8.1 The FFRs go beyond the minimum EU requirement on the grounds that they provide a higher level of consumer safety.
S1540 We do not have comparative data which allows us to assess whether the UK regulations actually lead to safer furniture than in the rest of the EU.
S1541 However, the EU standard is not seen as being particularly robust, is not published in the Official Journal of the EU and thus does not provide a ‘presumption of conformity’ under the General Product Safety Directive.
S1542 This would make it difficult to effectively enforce these EU standards.
S1543 We do have evidence that the FFRs are effective in saving lives and preventing fires in furniture.
S1544 8.2
S1545 This would be the most deregulatory approach, and would significantly reduce compliance costs for business since it would also reduce testing costs further and by definition remove requirements such as labelling.
S1546 As above, it would also help businesses who want to export to the rest of the EU.
S1547 This option would have the added benefit of removing the need to decide which products are within scope of the Regulations, thereby making the compliance regime significantly simpler.
S1548 8.3
S1549 However we would not propose this option as the potential risk to consumer safety, from less robust standards and enforcement issues, absent an EU standard is too large.
S1550 9 Conclusion
S1551 9.1 The preferred option is Option 1.
S1552 This will bring in testing requirements that more closely replicate current manufacturing processes, thereby improving enforcement as the new match test will better reflect the actual finished product.
S1553 Enforcement will also be enhanced by better traceability of products and greater clarity in the scope of the regulations.
S1554 The changes will provide the ability to reduce FRs in the fabric covers used whilst maintaining fire resistance standards by testing other previously unregulated components close to the cover in the furniture.
S1555 It will also allow for innovation in the development of new ‘protective’ cover fabrics and maintain the safety of upholstered furniture and bring long-term health and environmental benefits.
S1556 9.2 Implementation will be done by revoking the current regulations and replacing them with a new set of regulations which will make the legislation easier to read and bring greater clarity to business and enforcing authorities.

S2127 EXPLANATORY NOTE
S2128 (This note is not part of the Regulations)
S2129 These Regulations (the “Regulations”) impose requirements in relation to new domestic furniture, including some garden furniture which may be used indoors and furniture for use in caravans, subject to exceptions from some of the Regulations for certain furniture.
S2130 Some of the Regulations apply in relation to second-hand furniture.
S2131 The Regulations do not apply to goods for export or to the supply of goods made before 1st January 1950 or to the supply of materials intended for re- upholstering furniture made before that date.
S2132 These Regulations revoke the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 (S.I. 1988/1324) and its amending instruments.
S2133 Regulation 1(3) provides that these Regulations are to cease to have effect seven years after they come into force.
S2134 Regulation 4 sets out the definition of “furniture”.
S2135 Regulations 5 to 8 set out certain exclusions in relation to furniture made before 1st January 1950 and material for the re-upholstery of such furniture; furniture and materials supplied within the two years following the coming into force of these Regulations and which comply with the provisions that it would have been required to comply with the day before the coming into force of these Regulations; mattresses, bed-bases, etc.; and the requirement to bear a permanent label for second-hand furniture.
S2136 Regulations 9 to 11 prohibit the supply of non-compliant furniture and non-compliant covers, fabrics and filling materials.
S2137 Regulations 12 to 16 set out the requirements for furniture including the requirement for: upholstery to pass the cigarette test; filling material to pass the relevant ignitability test; permanent and loose covers and components close to the cover to pass the match test.
S2138 Regulation 17 sets out the requirement for furniture and loose covers to bear a permanent label.
S2139 Regulation 18 sets out the requirement for covers to bear an identification number.
S2140 Regulation 19 sets out the record-keeping requirements for manufacturers and importers.
S2141 Part 6 sets out miscellaneous provisions including transitional provisions and savings.
S2142 Regulation 21 requires the Secretary of State to review the operation and effect of these Regulations and publish a report within five years after the Regulations come into force.
S2143 Following the review it will fall to the Secretary of State to consider whether the Regulations should be allowed to expire as regulation 1(3) provides, be revoked early, or continue in force with or without amendment.
S2144 A further instrument would be needed to continue the Regulations in force with or without amendments or to revoke them early.

Proposals
S2183 2. BIS said that their aim was to keep the core proposals for amending the match test which they put forward in 2014, but make the different elements clearer in their functions of flammability and protection.
S2184 The Technical Panel had been convened to make sure that the technical aspects of these proposals, and the compliance choices for industry, are clear and workable.
S2185 3. BIS said the central objectives of the new proposal remain unchanged: to correct the weakness identified by Intertek in the current test and to reduce where possible the use of flame retardants (FRs) in meeting the requirements of the regulations without compromising safety.
S2186 The proposal should also bring cost savings for industry through a reduction in FRs and testing costs.
S2187 4.
S2188 The discussion then opened out to panel members and focussed on the various routes to compliance in the new proposal.
S2189 Looking at the balance of take up by industry between the various routes to compliance it was considered that industry would be very unlikely to adopt both a match test and Schedule 3 interliner route to compliance (although this is theoretically possible) due to expense; so this route would not be necessary.
Interliners
S2190 5.
S2191 Regarding the use of interliners to secure compliance, it was noted that at the time the regulations were written most cellulosic fabrics could not pass the match test so the interliner route was developed specifically for these fabrics.
S2192 Cellulosic fabrics smoulder.
S2193 Polyester fabrics melt but then stop smouldering when the source is removed and so provide a barrier effect which is not performed by the cellulosic fabrics.
S2194 Around 90% of interliners will fail the Schedule 3 test, as this uses a crib 5 (more severe than the match test).
S2195 The logic for a stronger test in this arrangement, however, was that the cover fabric itself was not expected to provide a protective function.
Re-upholsterers
S2196 6. Re-upholsterers could in theory use all routes but the high-end tend to use interliners to ensure compliance.
S2197 It is difficult for re-upholsterers to comply with the regulations now as they are often given a fabric by the customer which they then have to use.
S2198 For the new test, it was considered clear that they would find it difficult to meet the “components close to the cover” test as this would require them to take the item of furniture apart – which is not part of their current process.
Liners
S2199 7.
S2200 The use of a lining material is not explicitly referred to in the earlier 2014 consultation or the technical paper.
S2201 BIS wanted to know what role the lining material plays in the construction of furniture and whether / how they should be taken into account in the new match test.
S2202 It was explained that large retailers will buy fabric which they will then coat with FRs, have tested and sent to say 20 manufacturers across the world who will each use their own liner.
S2203 So to reflect the actual material used you would need to do 20 liner tests for each cover fabric.
S2204 Without that, the fabric provider and coater cannot then guarantee that their fabric would pass the new match test when used with a particular liner.
S2205 There was discussion as to whether the liners could be tested as a component close to the cover and there was some agreement in the panel that this could be logical.
S2206 Alternatively the cover and lining material could be tested together to assess whether, in combination, they provided a protective barrier.
S2207 8.
S2208 There are several different types of liners in use: cotton liners which could be used with feather fillings, polyesters possibly with crumb fillings and netting with foam.
S2209 All of these can be of different weights and varieties.
S2210 9.
S2211 Unless the liner is cotton then all of them will melt – the cotton will smoulder – and the liner therefore offers no protective function.
Innovation
S2212 10.
S2213 Concern was expressed that the regulations – because they apply in the UK only – could leave little incentive to innovate (i.e. in terms of reducing the need for chemicals).
S2214 It was noted that the sofas from one large retailer have different burning behaviours in different member states with the sofas sold in UK and Ireland having the highest levels of fire resistance (because of their stricter regimes).
S2215 Other Member states do have furniture regulations but they are for contract (non-domestic) furniture only.
S2216 11.
S2217 The innovation need would be removed if the need for brominated flame retardants was reduced or removed in the regulations.
S2218 This has happened in California where due to the ban on certain FRs, innovation to develop new and safer FRs has stalled.
S2219 There has been some innovation in coating fabrics.
S2220 Some manufacturers have used carbon and sprayed graphite onto fabric covers which were fire-safe but didn’t meet the requirements of the regulations.
Filling 1
S2221 12.
S2222 The panel agreed that Filling 1 could be removed from the proposal for simplification purposes, although this may not suit all manufacturers.
S2223 The removal of Filling 1 (ie testing directly over foam) makes the specification of the combustion modified foam (CM) less important as there will be an additional layer (the fibre wrap) between the cover and the foam.
Impact of the proposals on different materials
S2224 13.
S2225 The earlier testing for the technical paper which informed the 2014 consultation, showed that there would be a limited number of cover fabrics which would pass the current test and fail the new test.
S2226 14.
S2227 The introduction of a fibre wrap would lower the amount of FRs needed for synthetic fibres.
S2228 With polycotton mixture fabrics, more FRs would be needed to pass the new test.
S2229 But there is not a linear relationship between the amount of polyester or cotton in a fabric and the degree of burning.
Fibre wrap
S2230 15.
S2231 The current match test is generally perceived as repeatable but the introduction of a fabric wrap in testing could introduce an additional variable and it was felt more testing is needed to explore this.
S2232 The earlier testing for the technical paper used a single specification for the fibre wrap as it was focussed on reductions in chemical use in the cover, rather than the impact of the wrap itself.
S2233 16.
S2234 The panel then talked through the draft specification proposed for the fabric wrap.
S2235 It was mentioned that the technical paper put forward a hypothesis for the purpose and effect of the fabric wrap that was not necessarily representative of what happens in real life testing and needed further evidence – such as testing different fabrics with different fabric wraps – to support it.
S2236 17.
S2237 It was felt that ± 5mm was an appropriate level of tolerance in the specification but that this left a wide variation in the thickness of the fabric wrap that could be used in the test from 10mm to 25mmm which could have a noticeable effect on test results.
S2238 It was agreed that more testing would give a more informed view of the impact of different thicknesses and types of fibre wrap on fire resistance levels.
S2239 It was suggested that BIS approach the fibre wrap manufacturers for more information.
Cigarette test
S2240 18.
S2241 It was reported that there were many instances of leather covers failing the cigarette test.
S2242 There is also an increase in ‘fashionable’ fabrics at the moment that are failing the cigarette test The cigarette test was needed to provide a measure of the resistance of a fabric to smouldering.
S2243 The panel agreed that the cigarette test could probably be removed for 100% synthetic fabrics as they would simply melt away and not smoulder.
S2244 19.
S2245 The high tar cigarettes needed to perform the cigarette test are no longer available and it was suggested that a hot coil (suitably defined) could be used as a smouldering source instead for those fabrics where the cigarette test was retained.
S2246 This might also be more realistic as many smouldering fires are in fact started by other sources (e.g. overheating electrical chargers).
Protective cover
S2247 20.
S2248 It was explained that industry’s biggest concern is that some commonly used components close to the cover just won’t pass the simplified match test.
S2249 While substitutes e.g. for hessian could be found, it would be more difficult to find an alternative for e.g. the most commonly used webbing.
S2250 BIS felt this made it important to find a definition for a protective cover (or protective cover/lining combination) to allow a potential route to exempt the components underneath from the match test.
S2251 21.
S2252 The panel agreed that hole formation was not a satisfactory method by which to determine whether a cover is protective or not.
S2253 (This was in part due to repeatability – because the fabric would not necessarily behave the same way if tested throughout the roll.)
S2254 A list of protective fabrics would be difficult to keep up to date and would need to be specific as to weight and structure of fabric; but there was also concern that not many fabrics could be said to be protective The panel discussed whether other methods were possible to determine whether the integrity of the cover fabric had been compromised.
S2255 One possibility was whether a temperature sensitive paint marker could be used underneath the cover to test whether heat had broken though the cover fabric.
S2256 Alternatively, the cover could be tested over non-combustion modified foam (NCMF) as in the current match test - but this would mean a double match test – one with the new CM foam approach and one with the NCMF (the current method) which was not attractive due to cost.
S2257 22.
S2258 The point was made that it was important to test proposals with manufacturers.
S2259 Many manufacturers would struggle with compliance on their components – keeping track of what was on an “exclusion list” or not would be impractical Removing the list would mean there was not this source of confusion, but there would still be a struggle to comply.
S2260 It was noted that under the previous proposal, BIS had suggested the idea of a “protective” component which in theory would allow manufacturers to wrap their flammable components in cotton or wool.
S2261 The panel thought this would introduce rather than remove risk.
Conclusion
S2262 23.
S2263 BIS thanked the panel for their participation and concluded that they would reflect if any further follow-up or a second meeting was needed.
28th April 2016 Technical Panel
Definitions
S2264 1.
S2265 The panel discussed some definitions that could be used in the proposals or regulations.
S2266 2. Primary covers - a primary cover is the cover closest to the filling.
S2267 In the current regulations, pillows and cushions can be tested with primary covers to account for different types of fillings.
S2268 3.
S2269 Synthetic materials could be defined as non-cellulose based, i.e. not plant material based.
S2270 Any material that chars, will smoulder.
S2271 The panel noted that there are a great many natural/synthetic fabric blends, e.g. polycotton, and they contain varying ratios of cellulosic to synthetic fibre, with some containing enough cellulosic fibre to smoulder.
Stretch covers
S2272 4.
S2273 It was noted that stretch covers are very rarely tested.
S2274 It was suggested that a specific test for stretch covers is abolished and no difference made between them and permanent covers – any new test should apply to all covers, regardless of type.
Cigarette test
S2275 5.
S2276 The panel then revisited the proposal to remove the cigarette test for fabrics that passed the match test.
S2277 The match test is concerned with speed of ignitability and exposure to a high heat source.
S2278 Smouldering, as in the cigarette test, is based on long exposure to a low heat source.
S2279 These behave very differently, and therefore it doesn’t always follow that covers which pass the match test will automatically pass the cigarette test.
S2280 6.
S2281 However, it was agreed that the less than 1% failure rate figure for the cigarette test on visible covers was accurate.
S2282 And it was suggested that the cigarette test for non-visible covers is entirely redundant as there were no known failures.
S2283 It was pointed out that this assumes that the types of covers by manufacturers will not change with the new regulations.
S2284 Different covers might become more popular which could mean that the currently low failure rate for the cigarette test could increase.
S2285 7. Removal of the cigarette test would reduce testing costs to industry.
S2286 Carrying out a match test costs about two-thirds of the cost of combined match and cigarette testing.
S2287 8.
S2288 A new standard for a smoulder and ignition test could be developed using something other than a cigarette, for example, an electronic substitute such as cartridge heaters.
S2289 Or something specific to industry could be developed but this
S2290 would take time and money so might be something to be considered in the longer term.
Fibre wrap
S2291 9.
S2292 The panel discussed whether it was necessary to specify the density of the fibre wrap in the regulations.
S2293 There were mixed views as to how significant an effect this would have on the ultimate outcome (i.e. the safety of the product).
S2294 10.
S2295 Should it be decided to specify fibre wrap then 20-25mm density would be appropriate and wrap is usually defined by weight per square metre.
S2296 If density is not specified then the outcomes of testing could be affected, particularly at the extremes.
S2297 It would be appropriate to give a range for wrap weight rather than a point estimate.
S2298 11.
S2299 In the US fire barriers are used, e.g. fibre glass material, between the cover and the filling in order to avoid the use of flame retardants.
S2300 A fibre wrap as proposed in the test would not constitute a fire barrier.
Protectiveness test
S2301 12.
S2302 Development of a test to work out the protectiveness of a cover would be extremely helpful.
S2303 13.
S2304 The panel discussed some criteria of assessing when a test for protectiveness had been failed, such as charring of materials and the use of paint markers.
S2305 Fabrics that pass testing can still form small holes (dependent on the mix of synthetic and natural fibres) and split.
S2306 Presently, there is no measurement of splitting, testers just observe it.
S2307 It is not reproducible as fabrics do not always split.
S2308 14.
S2309 The California smoulder test measures smoulder time and char rate.
S2310 The latter is easier to measure than hole formation.
S2311 15.
S2312 The panel thought there were a number of options that could be used for testing the protectiveness of a cover including use of the existing match test for that specific function.
Liners
S2313 16.
S2314 The logic of testing liners is that it is material close to the cover fabric and influences how the cover fabric behaves.
S2315 17.
S2316 Type of lining material is dependent on filling and suppliers of covers do not generally sell liners as well, so it would often not be feasible to test covers and linings in combination.
S2317 18.
S2318 The work carried out for the August 2014 paper did not include liners.
S2319 The test methods used in development of the liner test had shown there was a question
S2320 about time limits for flame application and smouldering and whether these should be different or the same.
S2321 19.
S2322 The panel suggested that generally, liners should be treated like any other material that is currently unregulated.
S2323 All internal materials (components close to the cover), including liners, should be regarded as being the same for testing purposes.
Components close to the cover
S2324 20.
S2325 The panel discussed some of the issues relating to the component testing.
S2326 Components close to the cover need to be bigger than a match flame in order to be tested, so the size of some components is a problem for testing: e.g. plastic clips for springs which could be within 40mm of the surface.
S2327 21.
S2328 There is also an issue with re-sourcing components.
S2329 Some plastic components – if found not to comply - could be replaced by metal at no great cost to industry.
S2330 However, replacing elasticated webbing, which can be close to the cover and is not generally flame resistant, would be a problem.
S2331 Some flame retardant webbing exists but this is not widely available and is expensive.
S2332 This is probably the biggest issue with components close to the cover as work on alternatives would be time- consuming and expensive.
S2333 In addition, smaller manufacturers will struggle more with compliance if they cannot use ‘protective’ covers.
S2334 However, it was agreed that an exclusions list for components would be unworkable.
S2335 22.
S2336 All in all, more work will be needed to understand the implications of the proposals on components close to the cover, including wood which, as a natural material, has a variable reaction to flame/heat.
Traceability
S2337 23.
S2338 The intention of the new regulations is to give more detail about what must be supplied to Trading Standards and this will be in the form of a technical file.
S2339 It was noted that industry will need to develop guidance in this area.

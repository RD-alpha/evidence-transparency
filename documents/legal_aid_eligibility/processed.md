Ministerial Foreword
S0012 Legal aid is a vital part of our justice system and is critical for protecting fundamental rights and ensuring access to justice.
S0013 This Government remains committed to ensuring the legal aid scheme remains credible, sustainable and available to those who need it most.
S0014 To achieve this end, the means test, which determines whether a person qualifies financially for legal aid, must therefore ensure that funding is targeted at those most in need.
S0015 Under the current legal aid means test certain legal aid applicants are deemed to be automatically financially eligible for free legal aid if they are in receipt of certain income-based qualifying benefits.
S0016 This system is known as “passporting”.
S0017 Not only does it facilitate quicker funding decisions for those most in need, it avoids duplication, by improving information sharing across government and helps to reduce the administrative burden on legal aid applicants, providers and the Legal Aid Agency (LAA).
S0018 The changes to the benefits system currently being implemented under the Welfare Reform Act 2012 mean that a number of benefits that have historically been used as an administrative marker to “passport” applicants through the legal aid means tests to free (i.e. non-contributory) legal aid will progressively be replaced by Universal Credit.
S0019 In order to accommodate the introduction of Universal Credit, which was initially restricted to particular areas and types of claimant, the list of passporting benefits for civil and criminal legal aid was amended to include Universal Credit as an interim measure.
S0020 However, Universal Credit has a wider scope than the existing passporting benefits that it replaces, and it can be paid to claimants who are also in receipt of earnings.
S0021 This means that people who would have been in receipt of certain current non- passported benefits will, once they become entitled to Universal Credit, become eligible for passporting to free legal aid.
S0022 If Universal Credit continues to be a passporting benefit for legal aid, the increase in legal aid expenditure will be approximately £14m per year in steady state.
S0023 In addition, those in receipt of Universal Credit may be advantaged over those on similar incomes but not in receipt of Universal Credit meaning that legal aid is not focused on those who need it most.
S0024 Such additional financial pressure on the legal aid system is not affordable.
S0025 I believe it is vital, particularly in the current financial climate that the Government takes appropriate steps now to ensure any additional costs are kept to a minimum in order to protect the interests of taxpayers and the longer term sustainability of the legal aid scheme.
S0026 This is not a cost-cutting exercise, rather our intention is to limit any additional financial burden being placed on the legal aid budget whilst ensuring that means testing arrangements continue to make sure that limited funds are targeted fairly at those clients most in need and that clients who can, and should, contribute to the costs of their legal advice and representation, do so.
S0027 This consultation sets out the Government’s policy proposal in response to these Welfare Reforms.
S0028 I believe this proposal represents the best option for accommodating the expansion of Universal Credit to all claimant types into our existing financial eligibility requirements and also retains the benefits gained from continuing to passport certain applicants.
S0029 We would welcome your views on the proposal we have put forward and on any other aspects of the debate.

Chapter 1: Executive Summary
S0032 1.1.
S0033 This document sets out the Government proposals for amending the legal aid financial eligibility system in England and Wales during the roll out of Universal Credit.
S0034 1.2.
S0035 As set out in Chapter 2 (Introduction), the need to amend the legal aid financial eligibility arrangements arises from the progressive roll out of Universal Credit.
S0036 Continuing to passport all recipients of Universal Credit is likely to generate additional cost burdens for the legal aid budget which in the current financial climate are not affordable.
S0037 We believe it is important therefore to target limited funding at the most financially vulnerable.

2. Introduction
S0062 2.1.
S0063 Under the current legal aid means tests (described in more detail in Annex C), some legal aid applicants are deemed to be automatically financially eligible for legal aid (without a contribution) if they are in receipt of certain income-based qualifying benefits. ** (Reference: 1)
S0064 This system of using receipt of qualifying benefits, known as “passporting”, allows the Legal Aid Agency (LAA) and providers to make use of information already collected by the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) thereby avoiding administrative duplication.
S0065 These income-based qualifying benefits have been historically used to passport applicants through the income means tests for both civil and criminal legal aid and through the capital means test for criminal legal aid. ** (Reference: 2)
S0066 As a short term measure, the relevant legal aid regulations setting out the means tests included Universal Credit as a passporting benefit with effect from April 2013.
S0067 2.2.
S0068 The above diagram shows the legal aid qualifying benefits for passporting since April 2013 and the benefits that Universal Credit replaces.
S0069 As can be seen, Universal Credit replaces a wider scope of benefits than were historically passported to non-contributory legal aid prior to April 2013, specifically, Tax Credits, which includes Working Tax Credit and Child Tax Credit, and housing benefit.
S0070 Continuing with the current arrangements would eventually result in a significant pressure on the legal aid fund.
S0071 It is estimated that this could result in an additional cost of £14m per year for the legal aid fund. ** (Reference: 3)
S0072 Alongside the financial implications, if we continue to passport all Universal Credit recipients with income, we would be putting at a disadvantage those who may be in receipt of a similar level of income but are not in receipt of Universal Credit.
S0073 Resources are not limitless and the means test must ensure that legal aid is targeted at those most in need.
S0074 2.3.
S0075 We have considered how the current legal aid means test could be amended to accommodate the expansion of Universal Credit.
S0076 2.4.
S0077 For the reasons above, we remain firmly of the view that legal aid should be available to those with the greatest need and that it is right that people who can afford to should pay something, however little, towards the costs of their legal fees.
S0078 This ensures that finite public resources for legal aid are targeted at the most in need.
S0079 2.5.
S0080 This consultation sets out in detail a proposal for changes to the legal aid eligibility system to accommodate the expansion of Universal Credit.
S0081 2.6.

Case for change
S0093 2.11.
S0094 In order to understand our proposed changes to the passporting arrangements for assessing the financial eligibility of legal aid applicants we have set out a full description of the current legal aid eligibility rules at Annex C. 2.12.
S0095 As set out in 2.2 above, Universal Credit means that the current system of passporting those on specific qualifying benefits, including Universal Credit, will not be financially sustainable in the long term.
S0096 Universal Credit replaces a wider scope of benefits than are currently passported.
S0097 Continuing to passport to all those in receipt of Universal Credit would eventually result in significant pressure on the legal aid fund, estimated at an additional £14m per annum in steady state.
S0098 2.13.
S0099 In addition, continuing to passport on the basis of receipt of Universal Credit is likely to lead to a substantial disparity in terms of financial eligibility for legal aid between those in receipt of Universal Credit and those in low-paid employment who are subject to the full means test.
S0100 Universal Credit has been designed to support people back into work and is available to some households with average (rather than very low) incomes.
S0101 Universal Credit has been designed to improve work incentives by making sure that recipients will always be better off in work.
S0102 Universal Credit payments will be gradually reduced at the rate of 65p for every £ earned (63p from April 2017).
S0103 Universal Credit recipients may in some circumstance also be entitled to a work allowance (an amount that can be earned before Universal Credit payments are affected).
S0104 Therefore, Universal Credit recipients could be in receipt of household income that exceeds the upper eligibility thresholds for legal aid.
S0105 If we continue to passport all recipients of Universal Credit, some households in this group will have an advantage in terms of financial eligibility over those not in receipt of Universal Credit.
S0106 This does not meet the policy objective of targeting legal aid at those most in need.
S0107 2.14.
S0108 Therefore a solution is required that will make sure that legal aid is targeted at those that need it most and ensure minimal additional costs to the taxpayer.
3. Proposal for Consultation
S0109 3.1.
S0110 In developing the proposal for changing the legal aid financial eligibility systems we have focussed on the following policy objectives:
S0111  ensuring that legal aid is targeted at those who need it most;
S0112  efficiency in terms of information sharing across government;
S0113  no, or a minimum, additional cost to the taxpayer; and  minimising the administrative burden on the LAA, legal aid applicants and providers.
S0114 Based on the consideration of these principles, we have developed a proposal which is set out in detail below.
S0115 We believe that this proposal best meets the above policy objectives.
S0116 Passport Universal Credit recipients with zero income from earnings.
S0117 3.2.
S0118 This option would retain the existing system for assessing financial eligibility for legal aid with the following changes:
S0119  recipients of Universal Credit with zero income from earnings, would be passported through the income tests for all types of legal aid and the capital test for criminal legal aid. ** (Reference: 4)
S0120 There would be no passporting to legal aid for recipients of Universal Credit with any household income from earnings - they would be assessed under the existing income and capital tests, and pay contributions if assessed as liable to do so under the existing rules.
S0121 Money paid in respect of the Universal Credit award would be taken into account as income when assessing eligibility or liability for contributions;  recipients of the legacy benefits that already qualify for passporting, including State Pension Guarantee Credit (which is not being replaced by Universal Credit), would continue to be passported through the income tests for civil legal aid and the income and capital tests for criminal legal aid until they are fully phased into Universal Credit; and  all other legal aid applicants would be subject to the existing means test criteria and those on low income (but not in receipt of the passporting legacy benefits) may be eligible for non-contributory legal aid or be asked to pay a contribution towards their legal costs 3.3.
S0122 We are not proposing any changes to the capital and income thresholds for legal aid.
S0123 Neither are we proposing to amend the forms of civil or criminal legal aid from which a contributory payment may be sought or to amend the contribution rates.
S0124 Impact assessments and equalities considerations 3.4.
S0125 The impacts of this proposal for the Legal Aid Agency, providers and legal aid claimants are set out in the accompanying Impact Assessment (see Annex A).
S0126 3.5.
S0127 In accordance with our duties under the Equality Act 2010 we have considered the impact of the reduction in eligibility on individuals sharing protected characteristics.
S0128 These impacts are set out more fully in the enclosed Equalities Statement at Annex B.
S0129 3.6.
S0130 Legal aid claimants in receipt of Universal Credit with earnings from employment would not necessarily lose their eligibility to legal aid since they will be subject to the means test.
S0131 The vast majority of claimants would experience no change in their status.
S0132 A small number would move out of eligibility or be required to make a contribution.
S0133 A slightly larger proportion of claimants – would move from ineligibility into eligibility or contributory legal aid.
S0134 We estimate that around 650 would become eligible for free legal aid through passporting while 500 would become ineligible for free legal aid with 250 of those asked for a contribution.
S0135 This is a very small percentage of those who receive legal aid. ** (Reference: 5)
S0136 3.7.
S0137 Given the limited number of claimants that could be affected by the proposal if it is adopted we do not consider it likely that the proposed changes to legal aid eligibility would result in any particular disadvantage for those with protected characteristics.
S0138 Transitional protections 3.8.
S0139 We propose that transitional arrangements are put in place that would allow the amendments to the passporting process to be integrated in a way that causes minimum disruption for legal aid recipients and those administering the scheme.
S0140 Therefore, legal aid applicants who were in receipt of Universal Credit, irrespective of any earnings from employment, at the time of the original application for legal aid would not be reassessed simply because of a change to the passporting arrangements during the life of the case.
S0141 Special issues 3.9.
S0142 A number of special issues arise in connection with this proposal due to the need to take into account certain differences between the purpose and scope of Universal Credit and legal aid.
S0143 Special issues are discussed in Chapter 4.
S0144 Alternatives considered 3.10.
S0145 We have considered two alternative options.
S0146 These are:
S0147  To limit passporting through the legal aid means tests to Universal Credit recipients with earnings from employment of up to £500 net per month (£6,000 per annum) while continuing to passport recipients of legacy benefits.
S0148 This figure roughly equates with the potential earnings currently allowed to people in receipt of income support or income-related Employment and Support Allowance.
S0149  To assess income for all applicants, based on the current legal aid eligibility rules, with no passporting.
S0150 3.11.
S0151 While introducing an earnings threshold of £500 per month for passporting Universal Credit recipients to non-contributory legal aid could be argued to align more closely with the aim of encouraging people into work, this option would not remove the financial pressure on the legal aid fund, since it is estimated that it would cost the fund an additional £1 - £1.5m per annum in steady state.
S0152 It therefore does not satisfy the policy objectives of no, or a minimum, additional cost to the taxpayer.
S0153 It would also disadvantage those on similar income but not in receipt of Universal Credit and would therefore not target those who need legal aid the most.
S0154 Additionally, the Government’s preferred option appears the best in terms of the fit with various necessary IT systems.
S0155 3.12.
S0156 Similarly the option of abolishing passporting and simply applying the current means test would not meet the policy objectives since while it would ensure fairness and focus legal aid on those who need it most, it would not allow for efficiency in terms of information sharing across government and would increase administrative costs for processing legal aid applications.
S0157 3.13.
S0158 We consider that the proposal to introduce a zero earnings threshold for Universal Credit recipients is the option that best satisfies the policy objectives.
S0159 Q1: Is limiting passporting through the legal aid means tests to Universal Credit recipients with zero income from employment (while continuing to passport recipients of legacy benefits) a reasonable measure to accommodate the expansion of Universal Credit?
4. Special Issues
S0160 4.1.
S0161 In this chapter we have highlighted a number of issues that require special consideration to ensure that the proposal outlined in the previous chapter provides the right level of support for the various circumstances that have been identified.
S0162 Housing costs 4.2.
S0163 Housing benefits a legacy benefit that over time will be replaced by Universal Credit.
S0164 4.3.
S0165 In the current legal aid means test, the housing benefit an applicant receives (usually being paid direct to their landlord) isn’t included as part of the gross income calculation but is deducted from the applicant’s rent liability.
S0166 Rent (net of the housing benefit received) is deducted from gross income, along with a small number of other allowable expenses, to calculate the applicant’s disposable income.
S0167 4.4.
S0168 Under Universal Credit, housing support is not a separate benefit but is included in the total Universal Credit award.
S0169 The applicable amount for housing will be shown on the Universal Credit award notice, along with other information detailing how the award has been calculated.
S0170 Proposal 4.5.
S0171 We propose that applicants in receipt of Universal Credit with earnings from employment, and so no longer passported through the means test, should have the total applicable amount of the housing element of the Universal Credit award disregarded from gross income.
S0172 This approach would ensure that those in receipt of Universal Credit would not be disadvantaged compared to those still in receipt of housing benefit.
S0173 Rent (net of the total applicable amount of housing element) would be deducted when calculating disposable income.
S0174 Q2: Should the total amount for housing in the Universal Credit award be disregarded when assessing a claim for legal aid?
S0175 Contrary interests 4.6.
S0176 The current civil and the criminal legal aid financial eligibility assessments take account of a partner’s income and capital in determining the applicant’s financial eligibility and liability to pay contributions. Both the civil and criminal financial assessments make allowances for the increased cost of a two person household (and dependants where relevant). ** (Reference: 6)
S0177 4.7.
S0178 In civil legal aid the resources of the individual’s partner must be treated as the individual’s resources unless they have a contrary interest in the dispute in respect of which the application is made.
S0179 4.8.
S0180 In criminal legal aid, in relation to advice and assistance, the financial resources of the individual’s partner must be treated as the individual’s financial resources unless the individual’s partner has a contrary interest in the matter in respect of which the individual is seeking advice and assistance; or the Director of Legal Aid Casework considers that, in all the circumstances of the case, it would be inequitable or impractical to do so.
S0181 4.9.
S0182 In criminal legal aid, in relation to representation, the financial resources of the individual’s partner must be treated as the individual’s financial resources unless the individual’s partner has a contrary interest in the matter in respect of which the individual is seeking representation.
S0183 4.10.
S0184 An example of a contrary interest in a civil case is in relation to a claim by a mortgagee seeking possession where undue influence by the partner may be the client’s defence.
S0185 An example of a partner having a contrary interest in a criminal case is where the partner is the victim of the purportedly criminal act.
S0186 Proposal 4.11.
S0187 We propose that the position on contrary interest as set out above would remain unchanged i.e. the partner’s resources will not be treated as the individual’s resources if there is a contrary interest.
S0188 4.12.
S0189 Where a contrary interest exists and:
S0190 i) the household is in receipt of Universal Credit and neither party is in receipt of earnings from employment, both may be passported through the relevant means tests;
S0191 ii) the household is in receipt of Universal Credit and one or more parties has earnings from employment, their earnings will be taken into account separately; and iii) it is established that one party has no earnings, that party only may be passported through the relevant means test.
S0192 Q3: Do you agree with the proposal that where there are contrary interests both parties should be assessed using the Universal Credit award amount for their household circumstances but any earnings would be taken account of separately?

|  What is the problem under consideration? Why is government intervention necessary? The need to amend the legal aid financial eligibility arrangements arises from the progressive roll out of Universal Credit. Because of the differences between Universal Credit entitlements and those for other existing welfare benefits, continuing to passport all recipients of Universal Credit is likely to generate additional cost burdens for the legal aid budget of up to £14m per annum in steady state. Evidence Base
A. Background
S0272 Universal Credit was introduced in 2013 in some parts of the country, and is now available to single people in all Jobcentres in Great Britain.
S0273 Universal Credit is continuing to expand to all claimant types nationally.
S0274 It replaces six existing welfare payments: Working Tax Credit; Child Tax Credit; Housing Benefit; Income Support; Income Related Job Seekers Allowance; Income Related Employment and Support Allowances (referred to as "legacy UC benefits").
S0275 The Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) currently expect that the roll out will be complete by September 2018 and that the legacy UC benefits will have been phased out by March 2022.
S0276 Under the current legal aid system, receipt of certain benefits are used as an administrative shortcut for 'passporting' applicants through the financial means tests to receive non-contributory legal aid.
S0277 These benefits are: Income Support, income-based Jobseeker's Allowance, income-related Employment and Support Allowance and State Pension Guarantee Credit (referred to as 'legacy passporting benefits') and from 2013, included Universal Credit.
S0278 All legal aid applicants in receipt of Universal Credit have been passported since the benefit was introduced.
S0279 This was a short term measure, on the understanding that we would revisit this decision in due course.
S0280 Departments have been working together to consider how they might continue to deliver their passported benefits to those in receipt of UC as the benefit continues to expand.
S0281 To ensure these benefits continue to be targeted at those who need them most and that we maintain a transparent set of qualifying conditions that people can easily understand, many Departments across government have agreed to the introduction of earnings thresholds for the continued delivery of passported benefits alongside Universal Credit.
S0282 We are consulting on this issue now and this document forms part of that process.
S0283 Subject to the outcome of the consultation, any changes to the legal aid financial eligibility regulations legal aid would be made by way of secondary legislation.
S0284 We intend the new arrangements to be in place by Autumn 2017.
B. Policy Rationale and Objectives
S0285 Our rationale for the proposal is to ensure that we target our limited legal aid funds available to those most in need.
S0286 We also wish to ensure that those who do not receive Universal Credit but are on a similar income to those who do and are currently passported, are not put at a disadvantage.
S0287 Universal Credit replaces a wider scope of benefits than are currently passported.
S0288 The Government does not think that continuing to passport recipients of Universal Credit will be financially sustainable in the long term because of the number of additional legal aid claimants who would be eligible for non-contributory legal aid.
S0289 In addition, continuing to passport on the basis of receipt of Universal Credit is likely to lead to a substantial disparity in terms of financial eligibility for legal aid between those in receipt of Universal Credit and those in low-paid employment who are subject to the full means test.
S0290 Universal Credit has been designed to improve work incentives by making sure that recipients will always be better off in work.
S0291 Universal Credit payments will be gradually reduced at the rate of 65p for every pound earned (63p from April 2017).
S0292 Universal Credit recipients may, in some circumstances, also be entitled to a work allowance (an amount that can be earned before Universal Credit payments are affected).
S0293 Therefore, Universal
S0294 Credit recipients could be in receipt of a household income that exceeds the upper eligibility thresholds for legal aid that are applied to individuals not in receipt of Universal Credit, yet may automatically qualify for non-contributory legal aid.
S0295 If we continue to passport recipients of Universal Credit, this group may have an advantage in terms of financial eligibility over those not in receipt of the benefit and so does not meet the policy objective of targeting legal aid at those most in need.
S0296 To address these issues and in developing our proposals for changing the legal aid financial eligibility systems we have focussed on the following policy objectives.
S0297 These are to ensure:
S0298 legal aid is targeted at those that need it most;
S0299 efficiency is maintained in terms of information sharing across government;
S0300 there is no, or a minimum of, additional cost to the taxpayer; and
S0301 minimising as far as possible the administrative burden on the Legal Aid Agency and on legal aid applicants and providers.

D. Description of Options Considered
S0306 To meet the above policy objectives, the following two options are considered in this IA:
S0307 Option 0/'Do nothing': Continue with the current passporting arrangements.
S0308 Option 1: Passport Universal Credit recipients with zero income from employment and means test all others.
S0309 Option 0
S0310 Under this option all legal aid applicants in receipt of Universal Credit and those in receipt of other legacy passporting benefits for as long as they exist will be passported through the income test for legal aid (and the capital test for criminal legal aid where applicable).
S0311 All other applicants who are not passported would remain subject to the full means assessment. ** (Reference: 7)
S0312 Under most circumstances, the 'do nothing' option does not involve a cost and so is not explicitly assessed.
S0313 However, as was stated above, since Universal Credit continues to expand, and includes more benefits than we currently passport, more people will become eligible for passporting if we do nothing.
S0314 In the absence of government intervention, this will increase spend on legal aid.
S0315 Using our modelling approach, we estimate that continuing to passport all individuals applying for legal aid in receipt of Universal Credit will cost an additional £14m per year in steady state once the rollout is complete, compared to what we would expect to spend if Universal Credit had not been made a passporting benefit.
S0316 Note that due to DWP's roll out of UC and the billing of legal aid, it will take some time to reach steady state, but cost will be incurred progressively.
S0317 Sensitivity analysis of our key modelling uncertainty suggests a range around this of £9m to £20m. This is against our stated policy objective of minimising additional costs to the taxpayer, and our preferred option would mitigate much of this expenditure.
Option 1
S0318 This option would limit passporting through the legal aid means tests to Universal Credit recipients with zero income from employment.
S0319 Individuals in receipt of legacy passporting benefits will continue to be passported through income tests for as long as these benefits exist.
S0320 Note that State Pension Guarantee Credit is not being replaced by Universal Credit.
S0321 Individuals not in receipt of one of these qualifying benefits and those in receipt of Universal Credit with earnings from employment would be subject to a full means assessment.
S0322 Those on low income (but not in receipt of the benefits listed in paragraph 2 above) may be eligible for non-contributory legal aid or be asked to pay a contribution towards their legal costs.
S0323 Our best estimate of the net additional cost is around £400,000 per year.
S0324 This comprises additional cost to the legal aid fund for those with increased eligibility of £900,000 (para 29), and a reduction in spend of £500,000 for those with reduced eligibility (para 37).
S0325 Sensitivity analysis of our key modelling uncertainty places this within £200,000 to £600,000.
S0326 This option would reduce the potential cost to the taxpayer and ensure that the LAA continues to make the best use of information on an individual's financial circumstances that has already been collected by the government as applicants would not be required to provide the same information again.
S0327 By limiting passporting to non-contributory legal aid to Universal Credit recipients that are not working, it would ensure legal aid is targeted at those that need it most although those on low incomes may still be eligible for non-contributory legal aid since the current means tests thresholds would remain the same.
S0328 Continuing to use an administrative shortcut to passporting applicants through part of the process would continue to allow applications to be processed as quickly as possible and would be straightforward for providers and applicants to understand.
S0329 Legal Aid providers who have delegated authority to assess the financial means of applicants for civil legal help (i.e. legal aid for initial advice and assistance) will have to directly apply the proposal and rely on evidence provided directly by the applicant.
S0330 As this proposal meets all the objectives set out in paragraph 9, it is the preferred option.
S0331 We intend to put in place transitional arrangements that would allow any amendments to the passporting process to be integrated in a way that causes minimum disruption for legal aid recipients and those administering the scheme.
S0332 Legal aid applicants who were in receipt of Universal Credit, irrespective of any earnings from employment, at the time of the original application for legal aid would not be reassessed simply because of a change to the passporting arrangements during the life of the case.
S0333 Other Alternatives Considered
S0334 Two further options were also considered but do not form part of the consultation as they did not meet the basic objectives criteria: These were:
S0335 To limit passporting through the legal aid means tests to Universal Credit recipients with earnings from employment of up to £500 per month (£6,000 per annum)
S0336 (while continuing to passport recipients of legacy passporting benefits).
S0337 To assess income for all applicants, based on the current legal aid eligibility rules, with no passporting.
S0338 While introducing an earnings threshold of £500 per month for passporting would align more closely with the Universal Credit aims of encouraging people into work, this option would increase the financial pressure on the legal aid fund, since it is estimated to cost an additional £1-1.5m per annum in steady state.
S0339 It therefore did not satisfy the policy objectives of no, or a minimum, additional cost to the taxpayer.
S0340 It would also have disadvantaged those on similar incomes but not in receipt of Universal Credit and would therefore not target those who need legal aid the most.
S0341 Additionally, the Government's preferred option appears the best in terms of the fit with various necessary IT systems.
S0342 Similarly the option of abolishing passporting and simply applying the current means test does not meet the policy objectives.
S0343 While it would ensure fairness and focus legal aid on those that need it most, it would not allow for efficiency in terms of information sharing across government (means testing everyone would, in effect, be duplicating much of the work already done by DWP) and would increase administrative costs for processing legal aid applications.

Risks and Uncertainties
S0420 There is always uncertainty in the modelling of policy costs.

The Current Legal Aid Financial Eligibility Rules – Summary
S0581 In order to understand our proposed changes to the financial eligibility rules we have set out below a summary of the current rules for comparison.
S0582 This is a basic overview and is not meant to be a detailed outline of the means tests including all the different exceptions.
S0583 The framework for the provision of civil and criminal legal aid is contained in Part 1 of the Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act 2012 (LASPO).
S0584 The rules on financial eligibility are set out in section 21 of LASPO and regulations made under that section.
S0585 The rules on contributions to be made by an individual to that person’s legal aid costs are set out in section 23 of LASPO and regulations made under that section.
S0586 The relevant regulations are: the Civil Legal Aid (Financial Resources and Payment for Services) Regulations 2013 (SI 2013/480); the Criminal Legal Aid (Financial Resources) Regulations 2013 (SI 2013/471); the Criminal Legal Aid (Contribution Orders) Regulations 2013 (SI 2013/483); and the Criminal Legal Aid (Recovery of Defence Costs Orders) Regulations 2103 (SI 2013/511). ** (Reference: 21, 22)
S0587 Civil legal aid Civil legal aid provides support for claimants in civil disputes requiring legal advice, mediation or representation in court.
S0588 The Civil Legal Aid (Financial Resources and Payment for Services) Regulations 2013 (“Civil Financial Regulations”) set the rules for all forms of civil legal services.
S0589 Civil legal aid is not means tested for certain types of proceedings, for example, for children, parents and those with parental responsibility in care proceedings.
S0590 None of the proposals in this document seek to change the current exceptions from the means test.
S0591 Civil legal aid is also subject to a merits test.
S0592 The merits test to be applied varies according to the type of legal services provided and the category of law to which the application relates.
S0593 The means tests Where civil legal aid is means tested, the means test looks at both the applicant’s income and capital.
S0594 The general rule is that the resources of the partner of the individual applying for legal aid are to be included in the calculation of the financial resources of the applicant.
S0595 However, resources are not to be aggregated if the individual has a contrary interest in the dispute in respect of which the application is made.
S0596 In order to be eligible for civil legal aid, the applicant must pass both the income and the capital eligibility test.
S0597 Full details of how the determination of how much income and capital a person has is made can be found in the Civil Financial Regulations.
S0598 Gross income test The gross income test is an upper threshold, above which an applicant will not be financially eligible for civil legal aid.
S0599 This upper threshold is where an applicant has a gross monthly income which exceeds £2,657. An applicant who is in receipt, directly or indirectly, of qualifying benefits automatically satisfies the gross income test. ** (Reference: 23, 24)
S0600 This is called ‘passporting’.
S0601 Disposable income and disposable capital tests If an applicant satisfies the gross income test, account is then taken of the applicant’s disposable income and disposable capital in order to assess whether they are eligible for civil legal aid.
S0602 If an applicant’s disposable income or their disposable capital are above the relevant limits, the applicant will not be eligible for civil legal aid.
S0603 An applicant who is in receipt, directly or indirectly, of qualifying benefits automatically satisfies the disposable income test.
S0604 Those in receipt of qualifying benefits must, however, satisfy the disposable capital test.
S0605 An applicant who is in receipt of other benefits may have those benefits disregarded for the purposes of the financial eligibility calculation.
S0606 Subject to the exceptions described below, an applicant will be eligible for civil legal aid where their monthly disposable income does not exceed £733 and their disposable capital does not exceed £8,000.
S0607 The exceptions are where the applicant is seeking legal representation in respect of certain immigration matters before the First-tier Tribunal or the Upper Tribunal.
S0608 In those cases, an applicant will be eligible for civil legal aid where their disposable monthly income does not exceed £733 and their disposable capital does not exceed £3,000.
S0609 Waiver of eligibility limits The eligibility limits may be waived in certain circumstances.
S0610 For example, in certain proceedings relating to domestic violence, female genital mutilation and forced marriage the eligibility limits may be dis-applied if it is considered equitable to do so.
S0611 Thus an individual applying for legal aid for such cases may still receive legal aid even if their income or capital exceeds the eligibility limits described above.
S0612 Contributions Applicants who satisfy the gross income, disposable income and disposable capital tests may be required to make contributions towards the cost of their legal aid.
S0613 In respect of the types of civil legal aid, in some a contribution will be required from the applicant and in some no contribution may be required from the applicant.
S0614 We are not proposing to make any change to this split.
S0615 Work for which no contribution is required from the applicant: (a) Legal Help; (b) Help at Court; (c) Help with Family Mediation; (d) Family Mediation; (e) Family Help (Lower); (f) Legal Representation for certain immigration proceedings; and (g) Any other civil legal services as are the subject of a determination under section 10 of LASPO.
S0616 Work for which a contribution may be required from the applicant: (a) Legal representation (except for legal representation in the proceedings listed above); and (b) Family Help (Higher).
S0617 Contributions are payable where the individual’s monthly disposable income exceeds £315 or their disposable capital exceeds £3,000.
S0618 The requirement to make contributions may be waived in certain circumstances for example in relation to legal aid provided for multi-party actions of significant wider public interest.
S0619 Criminal legal aid There are a number of different forms of criminal legal aid, as set out below:
S0620  advice and assistance for individuals in custody – this type of criminal legal aid is unaffected by the proposals in this consultation;  advice and assistance for criminal proceedings;  representation for criminal proceedings; The Criminal Legal Aid (Financial Resources) Regulations 2013 (“Criminal Financial Regulations”) set the rules on financial eligibility for all forms of criminal legal aid. ** (Reference: 25, 26, 27)
S0621 Contributions in the Crown Court are provided for in the Criminal Legal Aid (Contribution Orders) Regulations 2013.
S0622 The Criminal Legal Aid (Recovery of Defence Costs Orders) Regulations 2013 provide, in certain circumstances, for a contribution towards legal aid costs at the end of the criminal proceedings in courts other than magistrates’ courts and the Crown Court.
S0623 Advice and assistance for individuals held in custody Legal aid for advice and assistance for individuals held in custody is not means tested.
S0624 When making a determination (which in practice is delegated by the DLAC to providers in this instance) regard must be had to the interests of justice (merits test).
S0625 “Interests of justice” is not defined, but in these circumstances it is deemed to be satisfied where a person has a right to legal advice at the police station and has requested such advice.
S0626 Advice and assistance for criminal proceedings Legal aid for advice and assistance for criminal proceedings is subject to a merits and means test.
S0627 Merits test When making a determination regard must be had to the interests of justice.
S0628 This applies for both the magistrates’ court and superior court venues such as the Crown Court.
S0629 The “Interests of justice” is not defined, but in practice will be deemed to have been passed by any defendant facing a custodial sentence and will be automatically passed by a defendant in the Crown Court or a Higher Court venue.
S0630 There is, however, a link to the Standard Crime Contract which requires that work only be undertaken where it is of “sufficient benefit” to the client.
S0631 Means test As with civil legal aid, where criminal legal aid is means tested, the means test looks at both the applicant’s income and capital.
S0632 The general rule is that the resources of the partner of the individual applying for legal aid are to be included in the calculation of the financial resources of the applicant.
S0633 However, resources are not to be aggregated if the individual has a contrary interest in the dispute in respect of which the application is made.
S0634 In order to be eligible for criminal legal aid, the applicant must pass both the income and the capital eligibility test.
S0635 An individual will be eligible for advocacy assistance where the matter relates to the individual's discipline in a prison or young offender institution, or in relation to proceedings before the Parole Board, and where the individual’s disposable income does not exceed £209 and disposable capital does not exceed £3,000.
S0636 In other cases, an individual is eligible for advice and assistance if that individual's disposable income does not exceed £99 and disposable capital does not exceed £1,000.
S0637 An applicant who is in receipt of qualifying benefits will automatically be passported and deemed eligible for legal aid.
S0638 An applicant who is in receipt of other benefits may have those benefits disregarded for the purposes of the financial eligibility calculation.
S0639 Contributions There are no contributions for this type of legal aid.
S0640 Representation for criminal proceedings Legal aid for representation in criminal proceedings is subject to a merits and means test.
S0641 The means test is different in the Crown Court and the magistrates’ Court.
S0642 Merits test The person determining whether the individual qualifies for legal aid must make that determination in accordance with the interests of justice.
S0643 There are certain factors that must be taken to account.
S0644 These are set out in section 17 of LASPO.
S0645 The LAA and HMCTS have also produced guidance on considering these applications.
S0646 Means test – Crown Court
S0647 In the Crown Court there is means testing against income.
S0648 An individual is eligible for legal aid if that individual's gross annual income does not exceed £12,475, or where their gross annual income is greater than £12,475 and disposable annual income is less than £37,500.
S0649 There is an upper financial eligibility threshold and so where an individual’s disposable annual income is £37,500 or greater they are not eligible for legal aid.
S0650 There is a hardship procedure to guard against this operating unfairly in an individual case.
S0651 Contributions
S0652 If an individual’s annual disposable income is £3,398 or less they are not required to pay a contribution from their income.
S0653 If it exceeds £3,398 they are required to make six monthly payments under an income contribution order of up to 90% of their monthly disposable income (subject to upper limits set on the total payable according to particular classes of case).
S0654 Applicants in direct or indirect receipt of passporting benefits are not required to make a contribution from their income.
S0655 At the conclusion of the case, an applicant who is acquitted is refunded any payments made under an income contribution order.
S0656 An applicant who is convicted may in addition be required to pay an additional amount from their disposable capital towards the balance of their defence costs.
S0657 While the individual’s income is considered for the purposes of the contribution order at the outset and during proceedings, an individual’s capital is only considered for this purpose at the conclusion of proceedings.
S0658 Appeals to the Crown Court from the magistrates’ courts in proceedings against conviction, sentence or order qualify for representation for criminal proceedings in the Crown Court without reference to means, but they may be liable to make a contribution to the costs. Liability to make a contribution of either £500 or £250 to the costs is dependent on assessment of gross and disposable income; there are no capital contributions. ** (Reference: 28)
S0659 Means test - Magistrates’ courts and certain committals to the Crown Court for sentence In the Magistrate’s Court there is means testing against income.
S0660 For an applicant to be eligible for representation for criminal proceedings, the applicant’s gross annual income must be less than the upper threshold of £22,325.
S0661 Where an applicant’s adjusted gross income is more than £12,475 and less than £22,325, a more detailed assessment to determine annual disposable income is carried out.
S0662 If the applicant’s annual disposable income does not exceed £3,398, the applicant will be financially eligible for representation for criminal proceedings.
S0663 If the applicant’s annual disposable income exceeds £3,398, the applicant will be expected to pay for their own defence in the magistrates’ courts.
S0664 There is a hardship procedure to guard against this operating unfairly in an individual case.
S0665 An individual who is in receipt of qualifying benefits will automatically be passported and deemed eligible for legal aid.
S0666 There are also certain age-related criteria which also confer automatic eligibility.
S0667 Certain other benefits may be disregarded in the calculation.
S0668 Hardship review In both the magistrates’ courts and the Crown Court means testing schemes, a hardship review exists.
S0669 If it is determined that an applicant is not eligible for criminal legal aid for representation in criminal proceedings and the applicant believes that they do not have sufficient financial resources to pay for the cost of legal assistance, the applicant may apply for a review of the decision.
S0670 Applicants may also apply for a review of a decision that they must pay contributions on the grounds that the applicant is suffering, or would suffer, financial hardship as a result of making the payment.
S0671 An example may be if the applicant has higher than usual essential expenditure.
S0672 Criminal - Magistrates' Court Other Courts (Recovery of Defence Cost Orders) Criminal proceedings that take place in courts other than a magistrates’ courts and in the Crown Court are not subject to means testing (the rules are set out in full in regulation 17 of the Criminal Financial Regulations).
S0673 The provisions on Recovery of Defence Costs Orders (RDCOs) apply in particular in the Criminal Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court and are the only means by which to recover legal aid costs from appellants in those courts.
S0674 An RDCO must be made, other than in certain circumstances, at the end of the proceedings and requires the individual to pay all or a proportion of the costs of their representation.

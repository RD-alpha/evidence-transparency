S0015 What is the problem under consideration?
S0016 Why is government intervention necessary?
S0017 The regulations (FFRs) have not been substantially revised since they were introduced in 1988.
S0018 During a 2010 Red Tape Challenge review two issues came to light.
S0019 Firstly, in order to meet the requirements, manufacturers use significant quantities of potentially harmful Flame Retardant chemicals (FRs) to make covers fire resistant to the required standard.
S0020 Secondly, weaknesses in the current testing regime mean that the testing which takes place (particularly the ‘match test’) may not actually be delivering the desired outcome (i.e. match-resistant furniture) for finished products.
S0021 Government intervention is required to resolve these two issues to ensure the regulations maintain fire resistance standards for all products.
S0022 What are the policy objectives and the intended effects?
S0023 1.
S0024 To maintain the current high levels of fire safety of UK domestic upholstered furniture, which prevents injury and the loss of life.
S0025 2.
S0026 To minimise the cost to business of meeting the flammability requirements of the FFRs.
S0027 3.
S0028 To provide options to reduce the use of flame retardant chemicals, on health and environmental grounds, particularly those on the surface cover.
S0029 4.
S0030 To give greater certainty to business on the scope of the regulations.
S0031 5.
S0032 To improve the traceability of products and aid enforcement of the regulations.

S0099 1. Background to the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations
S0100 1. The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 (FFRs) provide flammability performance levels for all UK domestic upholstered furniture, e.g. sofas, chairs, cushions, pillows, mattress fillings, etc. ** (Reference: 1)
S0101 While the FFRs do not stipulate any particular route to compliance, in practice manufacturers mostly choose to use Flame Retardants (FRs) as the most cost-effective solution.
S0102 1. The FFRs are successful in preventing injury and loss of life. ** (Reference: 2)
S0103 A BIS-commissioned report in 2009 shows that the current regulations were annually saving around 54 lives, preventing around 800 injuries and over 1,000 fires.
S0104 These savings to health and property were valued at around £140m per year. Therefore, any changes to the regulation have the primary objective of consumer protection and safety. ** (Reference: 1)
S0105 1. Enforcement of the FFRs is the responsibility of Trading Standards, with powers derived from the primary legislation, the Consumer Protection Act 1987. ** (Reference: 3)
S0106 Regular exercises by Trading Standards reveal that a constant threat to UK consumers is from non-compliant furniture imports, because most of the rest of the world provides little flammability protection for upholstered products.
S0107 2.
S0108 What is the problem?
S0109 Rationale for intervention
S0110 Testing regime
S0111 2. The FFRs have not been updated, aside from minor amendments, since 1988 and stakeholders, whilst strongly supportive of the FFRs, have often lobbied for an update. ** (Reference: 1)
S0112 One of the fire resistance tests, the match test, no longer reflects the way furniture is now manufactured.
S0113 For the past four years, BIS has been working closely with all key stakeholders on a review of the FFRs to update the testing requirements and provide a way to reduce the use of FRs.
S0114 2. There is growing body of literature that has linked health and environmental harm with the flame retardant chemicals (brominated, chlorinated and phosphate) used in furniture. ** (Reference: 2)
S0115 Furniture flame retardants can be associated with endocrine disruption, immunotoxicity, cancer, and/or reproductive and neurological impairments, lowered IQ, and hyperactivity.
S0116 Flame retardants migrate out of furniture, settle in dust, and are ingested by humans and animals.
S0117 Young children have the highest blood levels due to hand-to-mouth behaviour.
S0118 In the USA, a majority of residential fire deaths result from inhalation of toxic gases, and soot and smoke can obscure escape.
S0119 One study indicates that US fire fighters have high rates of types of cancers associated with dioxin exposure; the dioxins produced when flame retardants burn are believed to contribute.
S0120 There are some signs in the UK (from press and consumers) that consumer concern over the chemical treatment of furniture is growing here.
S0121 2. ** (Reference: 3)
S0122 The current flammability tests required by the FFRs are: match and cigarette tests for cover fabrics and the 'crib 5' test for filling materials.
S0123 Some FRs are needed for filling materials; however, these are largely non-contentious.
S0124 More potentially harmful FRs - particularly the brominated variety (BFRs) - are used on cover fabrics to meet the stringent requirements of the match test.
S0125 These are applied either by impregnating the fabric or by 'backcoating' it.
S0126 There is growing evidence that a) BFRs are worn away during normal use, getting into house dust and b) they damage the environment by releasing toxins and dioxins when burnt or dumped in landfill at end of life (see Annex 1 for evidence linking chemicals with environmental and health outcomes).
S0127 2. The current FFRs match test (see text box) requires cover fabrics to be tested over highly- flammable (and now illegal in the UK) polyurethane foam fillings. ** (Reference: 4)
S0128 This means the additional flame
S0129 resistance supplied by the combustion-modified fillings that are present in the final product is not utilised, leading to a higher than necessary ignition resistance standard in the cover fabrics (and more chemicals used to meet it).
S0130 More information is given in Annex 2 on the details of the test changes.
S0131 Box 1: The match test •
S0132 The current match test under the FFRs requires cover fabric to be tested over highly flammable polyurethane foam.
S0133 However, this foam would not pass the FFRs filling test, i.e. it is not found in furniture sold in the UK.
S0134 The EU match test, by contrast, requires cover fabrics to be tested over the same combustion-modified foam that appears in final products, which is the basis of BIS's new proposal.
S0135 The proposed new match test will be undertaken over representative actual foam fillings.
S0136 This will reduce the amount of flame retardant chemicals needed to pass the match test; without compromising the safety of the final product.
S0137 •
S0138 The current test serves two purposes: to reduce the ignitability of the cover fabric and to protect the filling material underneath.
S0139 The current test does not take account of the fact that the combustion- modified foam present in the actual product reduces the ignitability of the cover fabric and is also, by its nature, more protected than the test PU foam.
S0140 Box 2: The Cigarette test • We also intend to remove the existing cigarette test for materials which are required to pass the match test.
S0141 This is because the cigarette test is less stringent than the match test and many fabrics have never failed the test.
S0142 The main reason they do not fail the cigarette test is because they must also pass the match test, which is a much more severe test.
S0143 2. Government intervention is necessary to amend a flammability test requirement that no longer matches current manufacturing methods and has led to furniture producers and retailers using large amounts of flame retardant chemicals to comply with the regulations. ** (Reference: 5)
S0144 The new test updates the method to reflect current manufacturing practice and also introduces the potential to reduce the cost of meeting safety requirements to ensure furniture is fire resistant.
S0145 Scope of the regulations
S0146 2. Currently the scope of the regulations is defined by a prescriptive list of what’s in and what’s out. ** (Reference: 6)
S0147 The 1988 regulations give a list of inclusions and a short list of exclusions but this can give rise to ambiguities – for example, sofas with scatter back cushions (where the sofa itself would be covered by the regulations but the covers of scatter cushions could be considered out of scope).
S0148 We intend to clarify the scope and test potential exclusions (such as some baby products) through consultation.
S0149 Traceability
S0150 2.7 Currently the traceability requirements in the FFRs are limited to information about the product and the testing it has undergone.
S0151 What is lacking is information on the product supply chain which is key to effective traceability to ensure the safety of consumer products, by allowing product recall if a product is faulty and enabling the enforcement authorities to easily identify and locate the source of any problem.
S0152 Accurate record-keeping throughout the manufacturing process would aid traceability, and responsibility for safe products should be shared throughout the supply chain.

S0153 3. ** (Reference: 1)
S0154 The last Government decided to review the FFRs in 2010 in the context of the better regulation agenda, and this exercise was later incorporated into the Red Tape Challenge.
S0155 The review initially focused on three elements: the testing regime for flame resistance, the scope of the regulations and traceability of furniture products.

S0199 • Introducing flexibility into routes to compliance – the original proposal included an additional test to classify the fabric cover as ‘pass protect’ or ‘pass non-protect’.
S0200 Failure was to be determined by hole formation of the fabric.
S0201 If the cover was classified as ‘pass non-protect’ then components close to the cover would need to be tested for fire resistance.

S0204 4. The new proposal would allow the following routes to compliance for testing cover fabrics under the regulations: ** (Reference: 4)
S0205 Route  - interliner for cellulosic fabrics
S0206 This route already exists under the current regulations.
S0207 It allows for an interliner to be present in the finished item between the cover fabric and the foam filling.
S0208 All interliners need to meet the requirements set out in ‘Schedule 3’ of the 1988 FFRs.
S0209 Currently if the interliner is made of a ‘relevant’ material (eg is cellulosic as defined in Regulation 8) then the fabric cover does not need to meet the match test requirement.
S0210 Our best estimate is that currently 25% of products are produced using an interliner.
S0211 Route  - interliner + match test for non-cellulosic fabrics
S0212 Again this route is currently an option under the 1988 regulations.
S0213 Interliners can be used with non- relevant materials (ie non cellulosic fabrics)
S0214 but they then also need to comply with the match test, thereby losing the financial benefit (for cellulosic fabrics) of the match test exemption.
S0215 We expect that there would be a minimal shift to this route as it is likely to be the most expensive and estimate that around 5% of products would comply through this route.
S0216 Route - ‘Protective’ cover no need for components test
S0217 This is a new option.
S0218 For covers which are deemed to be protective, there is no need to test the components close to the cover for fire resistance.
S0219 This is less onerous than the original proposal that required hole formation testing on all match tests.
S0220 We believe that this route would only be likely to apply to ‘niche’ fabrics and estimate that around 20% would use this route though, having already consulted with industry, there are no figures available that breakdown the types of cover fabrics used.
S0221 Route - Non ‘Protective’ cover + components test
S0222 This is another new route to compliance.
S0223 The simplified match test allows for a reduction in FR use on cover fabrics.
S0224 Where these fabrics are deemed to be ‘non-protective’ then components close to the cover ie within 40mm must be tested for fire resistance.
S0225 Given current construction methods we expect this to be the most favoured route to compliance for around 50% of products.
S0226 4. We also intend to continue with the proposal to remove the cigarette test as confirmed by our further work. ** (Reference: 5)
S0227 Most fabrics which pass the match test will automatically pass the cigarette test
S0228 because the match test is more severe.
S0229 When the FFRs were introduced, this was not foreseen, and has become apparent over the years with the results of multiple tests.

S0231 Scope
S0232 4. The key question in relation to scope is whether certain products, or groups of products, should or should not be within the scope of the FFRs. ** (Reference: 6)
S0233 There is a debate about certain baby products (specifically prams/pushchairs and child car seats).
S0234 These are currently specifically included in the FFRs, but the Baby Products Association in response argued strongly for their removal as they impose a burden on manufacturers which is disproportionate to the risk (not least because of concerns about the use of FRs in baby products, and the fact that pushchairs and car seats are predominantly for use outside the home and therefore present a lower fire risk).
S0235 We propose to test this approach in consultation bye including an option that these products are removed from scope.
S0236 4.7 In addition, there are a number of ‘grey areas’ in terms of what falls within scope of the current Regulations.
S0237 These include outdoor furniture, scatter cushions, sleeping bags, and washable mattress protectors.
S0238 In general, we would propose that the rules here should be clarified in a way which seeks to maintain the status quo as much as possible.
S0239 The removal of products falling in these ambiguous areas would reduce the number of products which require testing and thereby the use of flame retardants.
S0240 In line with the approach taken in other product safety legislation (the so-called ‘New Legislative Framework’ or NLF)
S0241 we propose to explicitly remove second hand furniture from the flammability requirements of these regulations and their safety will be ensured through the General Product Safety Regulations as for the majority of other consumer products.
S0242 We plan to use the consultation to explore the appetite for removing these products from scope.

S0476 NON-MONETISED BENEFITS
S0477 5.58 The benefits of the proposal fall under the following headings: Consumer benefits; Health; Environment (including disposal); and Compliance.
S0478 Given the nature of these benefits and the uncertainties surrounding the behavioural response of businesses to the revised testing proposals many of these benefits are unquantified and may remain so even after discussion with stakeholders as part of the consultation process.
S0479 Each of the potential benefits is considered in turn below.
S0480 Consumer
S0481 5.45 There will be a safeguarding of improved fire safety for consumers as a result of a furniture testing regime that more closely mirrors the reality of furniture manufacture and is easier to enforce.
S0482 The existing FFRs have been successful in preventing injury and loss of life in the UK.
S0483 However, it has become clear that there are weaknesses in the current match and to ensure the continuation of those fire safety benefits of around £140m per year an updated testing regime is required.
S0484 5.46 Better labelling will enable consumers to make informed choices.
S0485 More transparency on routes to compliance for furniture businesses supplying the UK which may enable businesses to reduce compliance costs and pass these on in reduced prices to the consumer.
S0486 Health benefits from reduced use of FRs
S0487 5.47 Various researchers have shown that brominated flame retardants are present in house dust, from various consumer products such as furniture (see Annex for sources).
S0488 Traces of BFRs have been found in human blood, particularly children, and in pets, i.e. because these tend to be in closer proximity to house dust (see 5.3 and 5.4 above).
S0489 Other research shows long-term effects in rats from inducing BFRs at the post-natal stage, e.g. loss of attention and mood swings (see Annex for sources).
S0490 UK furniture retailers/manufacturers have reported that they are receiving increasing correspondence from the public concerned about the health effects of chemicals in furniture.
S0491 Environmental benefits
S0492 5.48 At present, old upholstered furniture ends up in land-fill.
S0493 Some of the FR chemicals present therein leach out into the environment and, according to Food Standards Agency research, get into the food chain (see Annex for sources). REACH ** (Reference: 11)
S0494 the EU’s chemical legislation, is proposing to restrict the use of decaBDE, a Brominated Flame Retardant (BFR) widely used in furniture production.
S0495 If adopted the restriction would come into force in 2018.
S0496 Defra reports that there are significant problems with the destruction of FR-containing products at end-life, e.g. with building waste.
S0497 BEIS, therefore, wishes to alleviate this problem by introducing an FR-reducing flammability test (as well as allowing for innovation in new 'barrier' technology that could eventually lead to a total absence of FRs in furniture).
S0498 5.49 BEIS acknowledges that in practice manufacturers will continue to use FRs at least in the short term, and that that means products containing them will still need to be disposed of safely at end- life (when forthcoming EU legislation demands it).
S0499 However, the new test will allow the use of inherently flame-resistant fibres which would not pass the current test.
S0500 We believe an increased demand for such fibres will drive down the price so that their use will be cheaper than FR treatment costs.

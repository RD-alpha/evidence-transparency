Foreword
S0022 The launch of the Midlands Engine Strategy is a demonstration of the government’s commitment to making the Midlands a powerful engine for economic growth.
S0023 Stretching from Shropshire to Lincolnshire, with the M1, M6, and most of our major railway lines running through it, the Midlands sits at the very heart of the UK economy.
S0024 The Midlands is also a gateway to the global economy, boasting Birmingham and East Midlands Airports alongside key ports such as at Grimsby and Immingham.
S0025 The Midlands is essential to our national economic success.
S0026 The region is responsible for over a fifth of the UK’s total manufacturing capability.
S0027 The services sector in the Midlands accounts for over four million jobs and is worth around £158 billion a year.
S0028 This is a dynamic region: there are now 427,610 more people in employment in the Midlands than there were in 2010 and the size of the Midlands economy has increased by £32.9 billion.
S0029 But there’s more that we can do to harness the region’s potential to help deliver the future we want for our country after we have left the European Union.
S0030 Building on our modern Industrial Strategy, the Midlands Engine Strategy sets out concrete actions we are taking to address productivity barriers across the Midlands, enabling businesses to create more jobs, export more goods and services, and grow their productivity.
S0031 Today we are announcing our plans to invest £392 million across the Midlands through the Local Growth Fund.
S0032 We will invest £20 million in a Midlands Skills Challenge to help close the skills gap between the Midlands and the rest of the country.
S0033 We will launch the £250 million Midlands Engine Investment Fund this Spring to finance the expansion plans of SMEs across the region.
S0034 And our Midlands Trade and Investment Programme will help position the Midlands Engine on the global stage.
S0035 Ours is an ambitious vision for the region.
S0036 The Midlands Engine Investment Hub is helping to deliver more high value foreign investment projects.
S0037 The first three Midlands Engine Trade and Investment Missions took place last year - to North America, China and Dubai - marketing the strengths of the Midlands on the world stage.
S0038 And we will publish a Midlands Engine Investment Portfolio later this month, to showcase the potential opportunities for investors.
S0039 Underpinning the Midlands Engine Strategy, and crucial to its success, is close engagement with local partners – Local and Combined Authorities, universities, businesses and Local Enterprise Partnerships.
S0040 The Midlands Engine Partnership, and its Chair Sir John Peace, have provided strong leadership for this work.
S0041 Sajid Javid, the Ministerial Champion for the Midlands Engine will continue to lead the work for Government.
S0042 Working together, we can achieve our ambitious vision for a Midlands economy that works for everyone.

S0051 Opportunities and Challenges 1. The government wants the Midlands to become a growth engine for the whole UK. ** (Reference: 1)
S0052 The Midlands is at the heart of the United Kingdom both geographically and economically.
S0053 The region encompasses 11 cities, several important market towns, and a range of economically important rural areas.
S0054 1.2
S0055 The Midlands has huge economic potential.
S0056 It is already home to more than ten million people.
S0057 Its economy is worth £217.7 billion - 13% of the UK’s annual output.
S0058 It has the UK’s second largest city – Birmingham – which is already growing rapidly: in 2014/2015, Birmingham’s economy grew by 5.1%. 1. Over the next few years, there are major opportunities to build on this base. ** (Reference: 3)
S0059 From May, Birmingham and the wider West Midlands Combined Authority area will benefit from a directly elected Mayor able to use powers over skills, transport and planning to drive local growth.
S0060 And HS2 will place the Midlands at the heart of England’s high speed rail network, making it an even more attractive place for people to live and work, and for companies to do business.
S0061 1.4
S0062 This region is already the headquarters for a range of globally significant businesses, including Jaguar Land Rover, JCB and Rolls Royce.
S0063 Last year, the Midlands accounted
S0064 for 19.1% of all English goods exports going to over 100 countries. It is home to a number of nationally significant clusters, including advanced manufacturing in the Black Country and Derbyshire, the automotive cluster around Coventry and Warwickshire, and the ceramics industry in Stoke and Staffordshire. ** (Reference: 4)
S0065 The Midlands’ science and research base is also strong: the region has 20 universities, with research strengths ranging from civil engineering to space technology.
S0066 1.5 Over and above its well-known manufacturing strengths, the Midlands Engine economy has a strong services base.
S0067 In 2015 Gross Value Added (GVA) in the service sector was £158.4 billion, 72.8% of total GVA in East and West Midlands. ** (Reference: 5)
S0068 The Midlands Engine Science and Innovation Audit highlighted that in 2014 the Midlands Engine accounted for 14% of UK GVA in real estate activity, 13% in business services and 9% in financial and insurance activities. ** (Reference: 6,7)
S0069 1.6 Of course, the strengths of this region are not confined to its man-made assets.
S0070 With seven Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty, two UNESCO World Heritage Sites and the National Forest, the Midlands is a great place to live, work and visit.
S0071 1.7
S0072 However, despite these strengths, the Midlands economy has been falling behind over recent years.
S0073 In 1997, the productivity gap between the Midlands and the UK average was about 10%. By 2015, it had grown to about 15%. This productivity challenge applies across both rural and urban areas. ** (Reference: 8,9)
S0074 1.8
S0075 The Midlands economy suffers from three key weaknesses that we need to address.
S0076 First, there is a shortage of skilled workers.
S0077 The proportion of highly skilled people is about 15% below the England average. One in every eight people in the West Midlands has no qualifications. And too many skilled graduates are leaving the region after completing their studies. ** (Reference: 10,11)
S0078 1.9 Second, the region’s economy is fragmented into small, poorly connected areas.
S0079 This means that the whole sometimes adds up to less than the sum of its parts and the Midlands isn’t making the most of its position at the centre of the country.
S0080 1.10 Third, there is a lack of entrepreneurship and economic dynamism.
S0081 In 2015, the number of businesses created for every 100,000 people in London was more than double the rate in the Midlands. ** (Reference: 12)
S0082 1.11 As we leave the European Union, it is more important than ever to ensure we build on the Midlands’ existing strengths, address these key challenges, and ensure the Midlands fulfils its potential to drive growth across the UK.

S0147 2.2
S0148 First, the Midlands’ geographical location at the centre of the UK gives it an inherent advantage in ensuring it is well connected to the wider UK economy.
S0149 92% of the UK’s population can reach the Midlands in less than four hours. 45% of British rail freight goes through the Midlands. ** (Reference: 13,14)
S0150 2.3 Second, the Midlands is already quite well connected to the international economy.
S0151 Birmingham Airport handles more than ten million passengers each year. East Midlands airport is the second largest freight airport in the country. ** (Reference: 15,16)
S0152 2.4 Third, the Midlands has comparatively strong digital infrastructure.
S0153 The UK average for superfast broadband coverage is 92.4%. In the East Midlands, 95.2% of households can access superfast broadband. In the West Midlands, the figure is 94.7%. 2. ** (Reference: 5,17,18,19)
S0154 However, transport connections within the region are often too slow and congested.
S0155 There were over six million hours of delays across the Midlands Motorway Hub in 2014/2015. It takes nearly three hours to travel just over 100 miles from Leicester in the North East of the region to Hereford in the South West by train - more than three times as long as it will take to travel a similar distance from London to Birmingham once HS2 opens. ** (Reference: 20)
S0156 2.6
S0157 The weakness of transport connections within the region is a particular problem because of the dispersed nature of the population.
S0158 Even though Birmingham is the second largest city in the UK, the Midlands’ population is considerably more spread out than most other regions of England.
S0159 This makes it more expensive for people, goods and ideas to move around, holding back innovation and making it harder for people to find jobs that match their skills.
S0160 Ultimately, this lowers productivity, and wages.

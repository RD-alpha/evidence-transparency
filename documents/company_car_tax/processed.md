1 Introduction
S0001 1. At Budget 2016, the government announced that it would consult on reform of the bands for ultra-low emission vehicles (ULEVs) in the company car tax system to refocus incentives on the cleanest cars using the latest technologies. ** (Reference: 1)
Aim of the consultation
S0002 1.2 Company car tax rates and bands, including for ULEVs are already legislated for until 2019- 20.
S0003 This consultation seeks views on the design of bands for ULEVs from 2020-21 onwards.
S0004 Box 1.A: What is an ultra-low emission vehicle?
S0005 An ultra-low emission vehicle emits extremely low levels of carbon dioxide (CO2) compared to conventional vehicles fuelled by petrol or diesel.
S0006 They typically also have much lower or virtually nil emissions of air pollutants and lower noise levels.
S0007 Since 2009, the Office for Low Emission Vehicles has considered ULEVs as new cars that emit less than 75 grams of CO2 from the tailpipe per kilometre driven (gCO2/km), based on the current European type approval test, and are capable of at least 10 miles of zero emission driving.
S0008 1.3 Developments in technology are bringing an ever increasing number of ULEVs to market with improved performance.
S0009 There are already at least 29 models on the market and many more are known to be in the pipeline.
S0010 This document sets out broad options for the design of new ULEV bands, which will continue to incentivise the cleanest cars into the next decade, a period during which we expect rapid innovation will deliver significant changes in the way motor vehicles are powered.
S0011 Views on these options are invited from a wide range of stakeholders including individuals, companies, and representative and professional bodies.
S0012 1.4
S0013 While views are welcomed on specific tax bands and rates, this consultation is principally seeking views on the general approach of how company car tax should be levied for ULEVs into the 2020s.
S0014 The Chancellor will continue to set specific tax rates as part of the normal Budget process.
S0015 In setting tax rates, the Chancellor takes all relevant fiscal, economic, social and environmental factors into account.
S0016 Therefore it must be emphasised that the consultation does not provide a signal of possible future tax rates, but simply asks whether the structure of the company car tax system is effective for encouraging the take up of ULEVs, and will remain effective given changes in car technology over this period.
S0017 1.5 The government will consider all responses before finalising the policy design and publishing the new bands in the autumn, ahead of setting any tax rates from 2020-21 onwards.

2 Background
Transition to ultra-low emission vehicles
S0027 2. ** (Reference: 1)
S0028 The government’s goal is that by 2050 nearly all cars and vans in the UK fleet should be zero emission vehicles.
S0029 This will mean that by 2040 nearly all new cars and vans sold will need to have zero tailpipe emissions.
S0030 2.2 Efficient transport is vital to the UK’s economic wellbeing, making a significant contribution to UK GDP and road transport remains the dominant transport mode in the UK.
S0031 2.3 However, traffic and new road capacity can bring with them concerns over urban air quality and climate change.
S0032 Road transport currently accounts for around one fifth of total UK greenhouse gas emissions with cars contributing over half of transport emissions.
S0033 2.4 The increased use of ULEVs therefore has a very important role to play in supporting mobility while reducing the carbon and air quality impact of road transport.
S0034 The increased uptake of ULEVs is part of the government’s wider clean air strategy and will help the UK meet its legally binding carbon emissions reduction and air quality targets.
S0035 2.5
S0036 The global automotive sector is undergoing a significant transition in the types of vehicles it is bringing to market.
S0037 Vehicles using electric and other new power sources are being built and sold in increasing numbers and manufacturers are investing billions of pounds into the research, development and production of alternative powertrains.
S0038 The government wants the UK to benefit from the employment and economic opportunities of this transition as well as from the cleaner and quieter towns which these new vehicles can bring.
S0039 Its aim is to support the competitiveness of the UK automotive sector as it makes this transition and for the UK to be one of the most attractive locations for ULEV-related inward investment in the world.
S0040 2.6 That is why the government has committed to spend at least £1 billion by 2020 to help position the UK as a leading market for ULEVs.
S0041 This money is being used to fund a comprehensive package of support for the growing market, including consumer grants for ULEVs to help reduce the cost differential between ULEVs and conventional vehicles, schemes for recharging and hydrogen refuelling infrastructure, grants for industry and academic research and development and a public communications campaign.
S0042 The government also provides incentives for businesses and the public to purchase ultra-low and zero-emission vehicles through a range of tax benefits, including company car tax.
Company car tax
S0043 2.7 Company car tax is a benefit in kind tax that applies when an employer provides an employee with a car that is available for private use.
S0044 Employees provided with company cars pay income tax on the value of the benefit while employers providing the cars pay Class 1A National Insurance Contributions on it.
S0045 2.8 There are currently around 1 million company car tax payers, and numbers have been stable for around 6 years.
S0046 2.9 Company car tax was reformed in April 2002 to a CO2 emissions based system to incentivise the uptake and manufacture of environmentally friendly cars.
S0047 The benefit represented by a company car is valued for tax purposes as the ‘appropriate percentage’ of the manufacturer’s total list price.
S0048 The appropriate percentage is determined by the approved CO2 emissions figure for the car as defined in terms of grams of CO2 emitted per kilometre.
S0049 The lower the CO2
S0050 emissions the lower the appropriate percentage and currently the system is capped at 37% for the most polluting cars.
S0051 Box 2.A: Example calculation of company car tax
S0052 A company car with a list price of £14,544 and CO2 emissions of 120g/km, 2016-17 appropriate percentage = 21%
S0053 company car benefit = £14,544 x 21% = £3,054.24, rounded down to £3,054
S0054 Employee paying basic (20%) rate of income tax pays income tax on company car benefit, i.e. £610.80 p.a.
S0055 Employer pays employer NICs (current rate 13.8%) on company car benefit, i.e. £421.45 p.a.
S0056 2.10 Diesel cars have historically been subject to a 3% supplement to the appropriate percentage to reflect the fact that they produce higher levels of local air pollutants such as particulates and nitrogen oxides.
S0057 At Autumn Statement 2015, the government decided to retain this supplement until 2021, which is when all new vehicles must meet air quality standards even under strict real world driving conditions.
S0058 2.11 The 2002 reform was not a single change.
S0059 Since then, the bands in the lower CO2 emissions range have been periodically updated to reflect and stimulate continued improvements in new car fuel efficiencies.
S0060 Until 2010, there were no bands which distinguished ULEVs from conventionally fuelled cars.
S0061 2.12 Over the last decade, radically clean car technologies such as electric and early hybrid vehicles started to become available.
S0062 To support this emerging market, in 2010 the government introduced new bands aimed at incentivising these ‘step-change’ vehicles, including a zero rated band for zero-emission vehicles and a 5 per cent band for ULEVs with emissions between 1 and 75 gCO2/km, which extended until 2015.
The current company car tax structure
S0063 2.13 To continue incentivising ULEVs and to reflect further improvements in ULEV technologies the government introduced two new ULEV bands from 2015: a band for the cleanest ULEVs with emissions between 0 and 50 gCO2/km and a higher band for ULEVs with emissions between 51 and 75 gCO2/km.
S0064 The government guaranteed that ULEVs would pay lower company car tax than the cleanest conventionally fuelled cars until at least 2020 to provide certainty for employers and employees as well as for the UK’s ULEV manufacturers.

S0085 2.15 Overall, basing company car tax on the CO2 emissions of vehicles has worked well.
S0086 The average CO2 emissions of company car fleets have fallen significantly from around 170 gCO2/km to 120 g/km over the last 10 years.
S0087 This compares favourably with fuel efficiency improvements for new cars across the whole fleet.
S0088 2.16 The UK has over-achieved against an EU requirement for average new car CO2 emissions to fall to 130 gCO2/km by 2015, with average new car emission now 121 gCO2/km.
S0089 This trend is set to continue as car manufacturers work to meet a further EU target of 95 gCO2/km by 2021.
S0090 2.17
S0091 The government believes that company car tax rates help to facilitate the move to cleaner cars.
S0092 In recent years around half of all new cars in the UK have been purchased by companies.
S0093 Business users are much more price sensitive that private users and factor in the total cost of owning a vehicle when making purchasing decisions.
S0094 This means incentives for employers and employees to choose the cleanest cars and therefore benefit from cheaper tax rates are more effective than for private buyers.
S0095 2.18 Company cars have a faster turnover than private vehicles.
S0096 The average company car ownership is three years, after which most are sold to the public, which in turn helps drive lower emissions across the whole UK car fleet.
S0097 The government therefore believes that the company car market is important for promoting the widespread use of ULEVs.
3 Refining ULEV bands beyond 2020
S0098 3. ** (Reference: 1)
S0099 The government is aware of the strong demand from key stakeholders for predictability in the company car tax system.
S0100 This is why the government committed to announcing rates three years in advance and has set out the rates for all cars up to the fiscal year 2019-20.
S0101 3.2 At Budget 2016, the government announced that beyond 2020-21 the main factor to determine company car tax rates would continue to be CO2 emissions.
S0102 This was decided following early discussions with car manufacturers and other key stakeholders.
S0103 The information is readily available and well understood by consumers and it represents a fair and simple structure to incentivise cleaner car choices.
S0104 3.3 As technology advances there are more and more cars coming to market with CO2 emissions below 75 gCO2/km including some conventionally fuelled cars like diesels.
S0105 By 2020 ULEV sales are predicted to represent around 3 to 7% of total new car sales.
S0106 Currently around 1% of company cars are ULEVs.
S0107 3.4 The ULEV category is defined by vehicles which emit extremely low levels of CO2 due to partial or full electrification, compared to their combustion engine counterparts fuelled by petrol or diesel.
S0108 This means that it is more difficult and less transparent to differentiate between cars on the basis solely of CO2 emissions.
S0109 This has led the government to consider adopting a secondary measure of efficiency in addition to CO2 for the post 2020 period.
S0110 Other definitions also exists that suggest 50 gCO2/km is a more appropriate threshold for ULEVs.
S0111 3.5 This is why at Budget 2016 the government also announced that it would consult on refining the bands for ULEVs in the company car tax scheme to refocus incentives on the cleanest cars using the latest technologies.
S0112 3.6 The government’s aim in doing so is to seek views on the most effective way of distinguishing between different types of ULEVs as the technologies develop into the next decade.
S0113 In particular, there is a focus on incentivising the uptake and development of truly step- change technologies over merely incremental improvements in existing technologies, for example the internal combustion engine.
S0114 Question 1: Do you agree that company car tax bands should be refined from 2020-21 onwards in order to provide stronger incentives for ULEVs?
What is the appropriate measure for differentiating between ULEVs in the company car tax system?
S0115 3.7 Company car tax has been based on CO2 emissions since 2002.
S0116 The government considers that this approach has been a success, since there is a clear link with the type approval process for new vehicles, and consequently it is easy for consumers to make simple comparisons between vehicles when making purchasing decisions.
S0117 Feedback from all key stakeholders suggests that the system is well understood, and that there have been clear incentives on companies and employees to choose vehicles with lower emissions.
S0118 3.8 Part of the reason why CO2 banding has been a success in reducing emissions of the car fleet is that the government has been able to set multiple bands that provide clear differentiation between categories of vehicles within the overall fleet.
S0119 The smaller the steps between bands, the greater the incentive for consumers to move down bands when choosing their next company car.
S0120 3.9 However, the development of new ultra-low emission technologies such as battery and electric motor technologies are starting to place pressure on the current banding structure in the ULEV category, which currently divides solely between ULEVs with emissions of 0 to 50 gCO2/km (predominantly battery electric and plug-in range extender hybrid vehicles) from those with emissions of 51 to 75 gCO2/km (such as heavier hybrid vehicles).
S0121 Advances in technology also mean that some conventionally powered engines now have emissions performance below 75 gCO2/km.
S0122 3.10 The simplest approach for responding to developments in the ULEV market would be to retain the current framework based entirely on CO2 emissions.
S0123 This would preserve the simplicity and transparency of the current system, as well as the commonly-understood methodology for how vehicles are categorised and the relative ease of administration for businesses and employees.
S0124 3.11 Many vehicles in the ULEV category now have fully ‘zero emission miles’ capability.
S0125 This is the number of miles they can be driven in pure electric mode without using the combustion engine of the plug-in vehicle.
S0126 The type approval certificates for these vehicles provide CO2 emission levels as well as the number of miles the car is capable of being driven in zero emission mode using the electric motor only.
S0127 The overall CO2 emissions value issued for these cars takes into consideration both the zero emission capability and the level of CO2 produced when the car is running solely on the conventional combustion engine.
S0128 3.12 However, not all of these vehicles have the same capacity for zero emission miles and this may not be reflected fully in the CO2 value alone.
S0129 For example, the UK’s top 6 selling ULEVs in 2015 had zero emission ranges varying from 15 to 340 miles but were classified into only two ULEV company car tax bands, with cars capable of 340 and 32 zero emission miles currently in the same band. The Office for Low Emission Vehicles is aware of a further 10 plug-in hybrid models coming to market this year as well as 7 fully electric vehicles and expect many more in the period to 2020-21 and beyond given that all major vehicle manufacturers have set out ambitious plans for more models and increased sales. ** (Reference: 1)
S0130 They also expect to see some more conventionally fuelled cars with emissions below 75 gCO2/km.
S0131 3.13 An alternative approach to differentiate between ULEVs for the purposes of company car tax would be to base ULEV bands on how many zero emission miles a vehicle can offer as well as CO2 emissions.
S0132 There are clear advantages to this in that it would be easy for consumers to understand and it gives a clear indication of the financial, environmental and driving performance which can be expected.
S0133 The Vehicle Certification Agency, the UK regulator, keeps a database of all information about vehicles, including the zero emission miles capability.
S0134 This would therefore be easily accessible to consumers for comparative purposes ahead of making any purchasing decisions, it is also a key piece of information in the sales process for zero emission capable vehicles.
S0135 There would also be consistency between this approach and the established framework for the government’s Plug-in Car Grant scheme.
S0136 3.14 Distinguishing ULEV bands based on zero emission miles as well as CO2 could provide different incentives for future vehicles with bigger and better batteries, which would permit most day-to-day journeys to be zero emission.
S0137 This would benefit both CO2 emissions reduction and local air quality in urban centres.
S0138 It would also provide a clear incentive for manufacturers to
S0139 move beyond vehicles with reduced CO2 emissions but limited electric mile range, which risk being driven in combustion engine mode most of the time.
S0140 3.15 Evidence suggests that pure electric vehicles capable of being driven longer distances before recharging are more popular with consumers, and that plug-in hybrids with a higher zero emission range will be driven further in pure electric mode, thus increasing environmental and cost saving benefits.
S0141 Therefore zero emission range is a reliable indicator of technologies, which meet government objectives and an important metric for the growing number of ULEV customers.
S0142 3.16
S0143 However distinguishing ULEV bands based on zero emission miles as well as CO2 is more complex than CO2 alone.
S0144 The revisions to company car tax since 2010 have resulted in battery electric and plug-in range extender technologies being incorporated into a single simplified table based entirely on test CO2 emissions.
S0145 This is consistent with other transport taxes such as vehicle excise duty and the enhanced capital allowances scheme.
S0146 Additionally, as zero emission range is one of the main determinants of a plug-in hybrid vehicle’s overall CO2 emissions level, this can be used as a simple proxy for mileage.

S0153 3.18 New ULEV bands could be structured in one of two broad ways:
S0154 a)
S0155 Either using a more continuous approach similar to the current system for higher
S0156 emission bands, creating a large number of narrow bands, which taper gently between 0 to 75 gCO2/km, or
S0157 b) Smaller numbers of wider emission bands similar to the current ULEV bands thereby
S0158 creating ‘cliff edges’
S0159 3.19 A structure with wider emission bands (b) would allow more flexibility in setting rate relativities between the different bands to incentivise customers to choose one band over another.
S0160 This could also provide more of an incentive for manufacturers of vehicles falling close to a boundary to improve environmental performance.
S0161 3.20
S0162 However, disadvantages of wider bands over the more continuous, narrower banding approach are that there is less incentive for consumers to choose the best in class car as the wider bands mean there is no fiscal incentive to choose a car at the cleanest end of the band.
S0163 There is also less incentive for manufacturers of cars which are not close to a band boundary to improve environmental performance.
S0164 Additionally, two types of car falling on either side of a
S0165 band boundary might be close in environmental performance but attract very different company car tax rates, which could be perceived to be unfair.

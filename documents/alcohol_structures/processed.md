S0001 1.
S0002 Introduction The government believes that alcohol duties should be related to the alcoholic strength of drinks.
S0003 This creates incentives within the alcohol duty system to encourage innovation by producers within the low strength alcohol market and create incentives for individuals to consume lower strength drinks.
S0004 In line with this, the government has already stated it would support any future changes to EU rules to allow duty on wine to rise in line with alcoholic strength . ** (Reference: 1, 2)
S0005 EU law requires beer and spirit duties to be directly proportional to alcohol content.
S0006 To encourage the production and consumption of lower strength alcohol beer, the government introduced an additional duty on high strength beer in 2011 and lowered the duty rate on low strength beer.
S0007 Cider and wine duties, however, must be banded.
S0008 This means that any drinks within a band pay the same duty regardless of their alcoholic strength.
S0009 A pint of 1.3% alcohol by volume ( abv ) still cider currently pays the same amount of alcohol duty as a pint of 7.5% abv cider.
S0010 The government wishes to examine options to amend the structure of the alcohol duty system so that duty rates better correspond to alcoholic strength.
S0011 That is why the government announced at Spring Budget 2017**(Link: 1) that it would consult on: the introduction of a new band to target cheap, high strength ‘white’ ciders, below 7.5% abv the impacts of a new lower strength still wine band, to encourage the production and consumption of lower strength wines Chapter 2 examines the case for a new still cider band targeting ‘white’ ciders, and chapter 3 aims to gather evidence on the impacts of a low strength wine band on innovation in lower strength wines and made-wines.
S0012 A summary of the consultation questions is in chapter 4.
S0013 The consultation will run for a period of 12 weeks closing at 11.45pm on 12 June 2017.
S0014 The government will announce its next steps once the consultation has closed and all responses have been received and considered.
S0015 Please send responses to the consultation questions to alcoholstructures@hmtreasury.gsi.gov.uk**(Link: 2) .
S0016 2.
S0017 A new still cider and perry band below 7.5% abv The government recognises that the majority of drinkers consume alcohol in a responsible manner.
S0018 However, the government is also aware of the harms associated with problem consumption of alcohol.
S0019 This remains a concern.
S0020 The government is committed to reducing excessive alcohol consumption and tackling the related health harms.
S0021 The current still cider and perry bands are: above 1.2% but not above 7.5% abv , with a duty rate of £40.38 per hectolitre of product above 7.5% but less than 8.5% abv , with a duty rate of £61.04 per hectolitre of product Public health groups argue the main still cider band – above 1.2% to 7.5% abv – is too wide as low-strength still ciders attract the same duty as 7.5% abv still ciders.
S0022 Theoretically, this can create adverse incentives to produce drinks towards the top of the band.
S0023 There is no additional duty cost from increasing alcohol content in a still cider up to 7.5% abv so the duty per unit of alcohol falls as the alcohol content rises in the band.
S0024 Higher strength ‘white’ ciders have been highlighted, by some, as a product that causes disproportionate levels of harm.
S0025 These drinks have an alcohol strength around 7.5% abv , and are reportedly typically purchased as a cheap form of relatively high strength alcohol.
S0026 A three litre bottle of a 7.5% abv ‘white’ cider contains 22.5 units of alcohol which is over 1.5 times the number of units the UK Chief Medical Officers have recommended for weekly consumption for those wishing to keep the health risks from drinking alcohol to a low level . ** (Reference: 3)
S0027 Public health groups report that these high strength products are closely associated with dependent, street and underage drinking primarily due to their low price.
S0028 According to Thames Reach, super strength drinks have become one of the biggest causes of premature death of homeless people in the UK.
S0029 The Alcohol Health Alliance cite research suggesting that 25% of alcohol treatment services patients in Glasgow and Edinburgh drink white cider, and 45% of white cider drinkers drink it exclusively . ** (Reference: 4)
S0030 The government has already taken targeted action through the alcohol duty system to support public health.
S0031 In 2010, minimum juice rules were introduced.
S0032 This means that cider or perry must, for duty purposes, be made from at least 35% apple or pear juice or else it is taxed as a made-wine at a higher duty rate.
S0033 The aim was to increase the cost of white cider by forcing producers to either: increase their juice content – which would increase production costs as fruit juice is typically the most expensive ingredient in cider to pay higher duty through reclassification as a made-wine In 2014 the government also took action on very cheap alcohol by banning sales of alcohol in England and Wales which cost less than the value of the excise duty plus the VAT on the duty.
S0034 This sets a minimum price below which alcoholic products cannot be sold.
S0035 Although these targeted interventions impact upon the costs and pricing of alcohol products, the government is interested in whether further action can be taken to continue to tackle drinks associated with alcohol harms, to encourage innovation in lower strength ciders and to encourage the consumption of lower strength alcohol.
S0036 Some argue that the duty rates for cider are too low.
S0037 A typical strength cider has a relatively lower duty rate compared to other alcoholic drinks of the same/similar alcoholic strength.
S0038 This is because the cider industry is an important part of the rural economy.
S0039 It uses almost half of the apples grown in the UK.
S0040 Any change in the main rates of cider duties will impact on a wide range of cider makers including those making lower strength drinks.
S0041 The government considers it is unfair to significantly increase the duty rates on all cider makers given the important role cider makers have in rural communities and the impact it would have on the many responsible drinkers of cider.
S0042 Alternatively, the government could lower the 7.5% abv threshold for the higher strength still cider band for ciders above 7.5% up to 8.5% abv .
S0043 However, lowering the 7.5% abv threshold would widen the higher strength band, meaning that there would be a greater difference in alcoholic strength between the weakest and strongest drinks captured by the band.
S0044 As set out above, duty rates for still cider and perry are split into two bands: (1) the main still cider band above 1.2% up to 7.5% abv , and (2) the higher strength still cider band above 7.5% but less than 8.5% abv .
S0045 Current rules allow reduced rates for still ciders below 8.5% abv .
S0046 Therefore, the government could introduce a new duty band below 7.5% to split the main still cider band into two.
S0047 The government is minded to introduce a new still cider and perry duty band to target high strength ‘white’ ciders, up to 7.5% abv .
S0048 All products captured by this new band would pay a higher duty rate than their current rate.
S0049 Those still ciders and perries in the lowest duty band would continue to pay the current duty rate.
S0050 Question 1: Do you agree that there is a case for a new still cider and perry band below 7.5% abv ?
S0051 The upper threshold for a new band would be 7.5% abv .
S0052 The impact a new band could have may differ depending on where the lower threshold is set: a narrow duty band (above y% up to 7.5% abv ) may encourage some producers to reformulate their products to reduce the alcohol content to y% abv or below.
S0053 The reformulated drinks would continue to attract the same rate of duty as now in the lowest band (above 1.2% to y% abv ).
S0054 Firms will need to consider a variety of factors before changing their alcoholic strength, including any impacts it may have on a cider’s taste a wider band (x% to 7.5% abv , with x% lower than y%) may be more likely to result in price rises for ‘white’ ciders and other ciders and perries that are in the band.
S0055 It may be more difficult for producers to reformulate their products without, for example, significant changes to a cider’s characteristics.
S0056 Therefore, producers may choose to keep their alcoholic strength unchanged, pay the higher duty rate and pass this onto consumers through price rises The government recognises that there may be conflicting views about the position of the lower threshold.
S0057 Any information on how the ability to reduce the alcohol content of ciders varies by the size of the reduction required would be welcomed.
S0058 Question 2: Where do you think the lower threshold should be set?
S0059 Please provide evidence to support your answer.
S0060 We would also welcome any evidence about reducing the alcohol content of ciders.
S0061 Question 3: In volume terms, how does the still cider market breakdown by strength in 0.1% abv increments?
S0062 While a new duty band would target high strength ‘white’ ciders, all still ciders and perries within the band would be impacted.
S0063 The government’s intention is to target ‘white’ ciders, and to avoid any impacts on traditional cider makers.
S0064 We would welcome evidence on impacts upon the wider industry to enable the government to effectively target ‘white’ ciders.

S0067 The impacts of a new still wine band between 5.5% and 8.5% abv The duty on wine and made-wine, like cider, is currently banded.
S0068 All wines and made-wines in a band pay the same duty.
S0069 The government would support any future changes to EU rules to allow the duty on wine to rise in line with alcoholic strength.
S0070 This could help encourage the production and consumption of lower strength wines.
S0071 In the meantime, the government would welcome evidence on the impacts a new duty band for still wine and made-wine between 5.5% and 8.5% abv could have in encouraging innovation in the lower strength wine market and encouraging the consumption of lower strength wines.
S0072 The current still wine and made-wine bands are: above 1.2% but not above 4% abv , which pays a duty rate of £88.93 per hectolitre of product above 4% but not above 5.5% abv , which pays a duty rate of £122.30 per hectolitre of product above 5.5% but not above 15% abv , which pays a duty rate of £288.65 per hectolitre of product above 15% but not above 22% abv , which pays a duty rate of £384.82 per hectolitre of product above 22% abv , which pays a duty rate of £28.74 per litre of pure alcohol EU law requires that there has to be a single band for wine between 8.5% and 15% abv .
S0073 As the UK’s main still wine and made-wine band is currently 5.5% to 15% abv , it would be possible to split the main still wine and made-wine band into two.
S0074 Under this scenario, there would be new duty bands for wines and made-wines between 5.5% and 8.5% abv with a lower duty rate than currently applied.
S0075 The government has no current plans to change the duty rate on wines between 8.5% and 15% abv apart from those assumed in the public finances.
S0076 This would mimic the duty structure for sparkling wine and made-wines, which has different rates depending on whether the sparkling wine is either above 5.5% up to 8.5% abv or between 8.5% and 15% abv .
S0077 Question 5: Would a new band for still wine above 5.5% up to 8.5% abv encourage innovation in the lower strength wine market?
S0078 It is possible that changes to the structure of the alcohol duty system could encourage innovation in the low strength wine market.
S0079 Nevertheless, the government appreciates that there may be other non-tax barriers to growth for the lower strength wine market, for example wider regulations or the attitude and tastes of consumers.
S0080 Question 6: We would welcome evidence on non-tax barriers to the growth of the lower-strength wine market.
S0081 As set out above, any new duty band for low strength still wine and made-wine could have twin objectives, namely to encourage: consumers to buy lower alcohol wine.
S0082 Our initial research shows some still wines and made-wines below 8.5% abv (e.g. rosé and fruit wines) can be found in a wide range of retailers producers to reformulate their drinks given the number of products just above 8.5% abv .
S0083 Lowering alcohol content offers health benefits for drinkers The government has limited evidence on whether a new duty band would help fulfil these objectives.
S0084 For example, we have limited data on this area of the wine market.
S0085 The government would welcome evidence on the proportion of total sales within the wine market accounted for by lower strength wines and how this market is expected to develop in the future.
S0086 Also, we have limited evidence on reformulating wine and made-wine drinks.
S0087 Question 7: We would welcome evidence on the current and future performance of the lower-strength wine and made-wine markets, including information on volumes sold.
S0088 Question 8: We would also welcome evidence on the practicalities of reformulation for wine and made-wine producers.
S0089 The government is aware that a new band for still wine between 5.5% and 8.5% abv would impact upon a wide range of consumers and organisations.
S0090 Question 9: The government would welcome evidence on the impacts of introducing a new band on: businesses consumers public health Wine and made-wine are currently subject to the same duty rates, and historically always have been.
S0091 If this continued, a new band would also be introduced for made-wine above 5.5% up to 8.5% abv .
S0092 Made-wines are drinks like fruit wines, fruit ciders, some alcopops, mead and nettle beer.
S0093 Question 10: If the government decides to introduce a new still wine band, should the new duty band also be applied to still made-wines?
S0094 Question 11: What impacts would a new still made-wine band have?
S0095 The introduction of a new wine or made-wine band would lessen the duty impact if a producer of a 5.5% abv drink chooses to increase the alcohol content of their drink.
S0096 This could create adverse incentives for some producers to increase their alcohol strength, contradicting the government’s aim to encourage innovation in lower-strength wines.
S0097 Question 12: Do you think introducing a new still wine and made-wine band could create adverse incentives for producers to increase their alcohol strength of some of their drinks?
S0098 If so, how large an effect would you expect this to be?

S0118 5.
S0119 Consultation process This consultation is being conducted in line with the tax consultation framework.
S0120 There are 5 stages to tax policy development: Stage 1 – Setting out objectives and identifying options Stage 2 – Determining the best option and developing a framework for implementation including detailed policy design Stage 3 – Drafting legislation to effect the proposed change Stage 4 – Implementing and monitoring the change Stage 5 – Reviewing and evaluating the change This consultation is taking place during stage 1 of the process.
S0121 The purpose of the consultation is to seek views on the policy design and any suitable possible alternatives.
